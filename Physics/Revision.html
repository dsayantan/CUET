ConceptCharge, Coulomb’s law, superposition

  • Charge is quantised (q = ne, because charge is transferred by whole electrons), conserved in an isolated system, additive (a scalar), and the same in every frame of reference. Rubbing transfers electrons: the body that loses them becomes positive.
  • Coulomb: F = kq1q2/r2 along the line joining them, k = 9 × 109 N m2 C−2. In a medium of dielectric constant K the force is F/K. The force between two charges is not changed by a third charge: the net force on one is the vector sum (superposition).
  • Two identical conducting spheres touched together share the total charge equally (algebraic sum, so +4 and −2 give +1 each).
  • A charge Q split into q and Q − q repels most when q = Q/2.
  • Field lines start on + and end on − charges, never cross, never form closed loops (electrostatic field is conservative), and are crowded where E is strong. They meet a conductor at right angles.

ConceptElectric field, dipole, flux and Gauss’s law

  • E = F/q0; for a point charge E = kq/r2, so doubling the charge doubles E.
  • Dipole: charges ±q a distance 2a apart, p = q(2a) from −q to +q. Its total charge is zero. Far away its field falls as 1/r3. In a uniform field: net force zero, torque τ = p × E, energy U = −p·E. Stable when p is along E (U = −pE), unstable when opposite (U = +pE); turning it from stable to unstable needs work 2pE. In a non-uniform field with p along E there is a net force (towards stronger field) and no torque.
  • Flux Φ = E·A (N m2/C = V m). Gauss: total flux out of any closed surface = qenclosed/ε0, whatever the shape or size of the surface and wherever the charge sits inside. Charges outside add zero net flux (but do change E on the surface).
  • Symmetry tricks: charge at the centre of a cube → q/6ε0 per face; at the centre of a face → q/2ε0 out of the cube; at a corner → q/8ε0; on the midpoint of an edge → q/4ε0.
  • Inside a conductor in equilibrium E = 0, all excess charge sits on the outer surface, and E just outside is σ/ε0, normal to the surface. A charge q at the centre of a conducting shell carrying Q puts −q on the inner surface and Q + q on the outer.

DerivationField of a dipole on the axis and on the equator

Dipole −q at A, +q at B, AB = 2a, centre O.

  1. Axial point P at distance r from O on the side of +q: E+ = kq/(r − a)2 away from B, E− = kq/(r + a)2 towards A. Both lie on the axis, opposite ways, and E+ is larger.
  2. E = kq[1/(r−a)2 − 1/(r+a)2] = kq·4ar/(r2−a2)2 = 2kpr/(r2−a2)2 → 2kp/r3 for r » a, along p.
  3. Equatorial point Q at distance r on the bisector: each charge is √(r2+a2) away, so |E+| = |E−| = kq/(r2+a2).
  4. Components perpendicular to the axis cancel; components parallel to the axis add, each = E+·a/√(r2+a2), both pointing from + to −.
  5. E = 2kqa/(r2+a2)3/2 = kp/(r2+a2)3/2 → kp/r3, directed opposite to p.
  6. Ratio at the same large distance: Eaxial : Eequatorial = 2 : 1.

DerivationGauss’s law applications: line, sheet, shell

Pick a surface on which E is either constant and normal, or parallel (no flux).

  1. Line charge λ: coaxial cylinder of radius r and length l. Flux only through the curved face: E·2πrl = λl/ε0 ⇒ E = λ/2πε0r.
  2. Plane sheet σ: pill box of face area A through the sheet. Flux through both faces: 2EA = σA/ε0 ⇒ E = σ/2ε0, the same at every distance.
  3. Thin shell Q, radius R: concentric sphere of radius r. Outside, E·4πr2 = Q/ε0 ⇒ E = kQ/r2, as if all the charge sat at the centre. Inside, the sphere encloses no charge ⇒ E = 0.
  4. Two parallel sheets +σ and −σ: each gives σ/2ε0; they add between the sheets (σ/ε0) and cancel outside.

Worked exampleTouch and separate

Two identical small conducting spheres carry +4 μC and −2 μC and attract each other with force F. They are touched together and put back at the same separation. The new force is

  1. (1) F/8, attractive
  2. (2) F/8, repulsive
  3. (3) F/4, repulsive
  4. (4) 9F/8, repulsive
  1. After contact each carries (+4 − 2)/2 = +1 μC, so the force is now repulsive.
  2. F ∝ q1q2: before |4 × 2| = 8, after 1 × 1 = 1, so F′ = F/8.

Answer: (2) F/8, repulsive

Worked exampleWhere is the field zero?

Charges q1 = +16 μC and q2 = −4 μC are 20 cm apart. The point on the line joining them where the electric field is zero lies

  1. (1) 8 cm from q1, between them
  2. (2) 40 cm from q1, beyond q2
  3. (3) 13.3 cm from q1, between them
  4. (4) 20 cm from q1, beyond q1
  1. Unlike charges: between them both fields point the same way (towards q2), so the null point is outside, on the side of the smaller charge.

Worked exampleDipole statements

For an electric dipole in a uniform field, which statements are correct? (A) The net force on it is zero. (B) The torque is maximum when p ⊥ E. (C) The potential energy is minimum when p is antiparallel to E. (D) The work needed to turn it from the stable to the unstable position is 2pE.

  1. (1) (A), (B) and (D) only
  2. (2) (A) and (C) only
  3. (3) (B), (C) and (D) only
  4. (4) (A), (B), (C) and (D)
  1. (A) +qE and −qE cancel. (B) τ = pE sinθ is largest at 90°.
  2. (C) is false: U = −pE cosθ is minimum (−pE) when p is along E.
  3. (D) W = U(180°) − U(0°) = pE − (−pE) = 2pE.

Answer: (1) (A), (B) and (D) only

TrapsTraps

  • Charge shared on contact is the algebraic sum halved; signs matter.
  • For unlike charges the null point is outside, nearer the smaller magnitude; for like charges it is between, nearer the smaller.
  • Gauss’s law gives the total flux; it does not say E is the same everywhere on the surface. A charge outside contributes zero net flux.
  • Sheet field σ/2ε0, conductor surface field σ/ε0: do not mix them up.
  • The dipole’s equatorial field points opposite to p, not along it.
  • Unit of flux in SI base units: kg m3 s−3 A−1 (N m2/C written out).

ConceptPotential and potential energy

  • V = W/q0 (work per unit charge to bring a test charge from infinity). Point charge V = kq/r, a scalar: add potentials algebraically. At the centroid of 2q, −q, −q the potential is zero but the field is not.
  • E = −dV/dr (potential gradient): E points the way V falls fastest. In a uniform field VA − VB = E·(xB − xA) along the field.
  • Equipotential surfaces are perpendicular to field lines; no work is done moving a charge along one; two never intersect; closer spacing means stronger field. A conductor is an equipotential, so V inside a charged conductor = V on its surface while E inside = 0.
  • Dipole potential V = kp cosθ/r2: depends on r and θ; zero everywhere on the equatorial plane.
  • Energy of a pair U = kq1q2/r (negative for unlike charges, i.e. bound). For three charges add the three pairs. Work needed to pull a pair apart to infinity = −U.

ConceptCapacitors and dielectrics

  • C = Q/V depends only on geometry and the medium, not on Q or V. Parallel plate C = Kε0A/d; isolated sphere C = 4πε0R (so 1 pF needs R = 0.9 cm). A capacitor blocks steady d.c. once charged: current zero.
  • Energy U = ½CV2 = Q2/2C = ½QV, stored in the field with density u = ½ε0E2.
  • A dielectric is polarised: induced surface charges oppose the applied field, so E inside drops by K. Free charges do not move in it.
  • Slab inserted with the battery connected: V fixed, C × K, Q × K, U × K. With the battery disconnected: Q fixed, C × K, V ÷ K, E ÷ K, U ÷ K.
  • Two capacitors joined (like plates together) reach a common potential V = (C1V1 + C2V2)/(C1 + C2); energy is always lost: ΔU = C1C2(V1 − V2)2/2(C1+C2). Spheres of radii R1, R2 touched share charge in the ratio R1 : R2 (C ∝ R).

DerivationParallel-plate capacitor, with and without a dielectric slab

Plates of area A, separation d, charges ±Q, surface density σ = Q/A.

  1. Field between the plates E0 = σ/ε0, uniform. V = E0d = Qd/ε0A, so C = Q/V = ε0A/d.
  2. Insert a slab of thickness t and constant K. Inside it the field is E0/K; in the remaining air gap (d − t) it is still E0.
  3. V = E0(d − t) + (E0/K)t = (Q/ε0A)(d − t + t/K).
  4. C = ε0A/(d − t + t/K). Slab fills the gap (t = d): C = Kε0A/d. Metal slab (K → ∞): C = ε0A/(d − t), independent of where the slab sits.
  5. Energy: charging adds dq at potential q/C, so U = ∫q dq/C = Q2/2C. Dividing by the volume Ad gives u = ½ε0E2.

TrapsTraps

  • Battery connected or disconnected? Decide that first; it decides which of Q and V stays fixed.
  • Potential is a scalar: at the centre of a square of charges +q, +q, −q, −q, V = 0 but E need not be.
  • Inside a charged conducting sphere E = 0 but V = kQ/R, not zero.
  • Work done by the field = −ΔU; work done by you = +ΔU.
  • Capacitors in series: smaller C takes the larger voltage. Resistors in series: larger R takes the larger voltage.
  • The metal-slab result C = ε0A/(d − t) does not depend on the slab’s position.

ConceptCurrent, drift and resistance

  • I = dq/dt. If q(t) or I(t) is given, differentiate or integrate (charge = area under I–t graph).
  • Electrons accelerate in E between collisions and gain a small average drift velocity vd = eEτ/m (mm/s). The circuit responds at once because the field is set up at nearly the speed of light, not because electrons race round.
  • I = neAvd. Along a wire of varying cross-section the current is the same; vd, J and E are larger where it is thinner.
  • Mobility μ = vd/E = eτ/m. Ohm’s law in vector form J = σE, with ρ = 1/σ = m/ne2τ.
  • R = ρl/A: ρ depends only on the material and temperature. Stretching to n times the length (volume fixed) makes R × n2.
  • Metals: ρ rises with T (τ falls). Semiconductors: ρ falls with T (n rises). Alloys (nichrome, manganin, constantan): high ρ, tiny temperature coefficient: used for standard resistors, bridge wires and heaters (nichrome also has a high melting point and does not oxidise).
  • Colour code: B B R O Y G B V G W = 0–9; third band is the power of ten; gold ±5%, silver ±10%.

ConceptCells, Kirchhoff, power

  • A cell of emf ε and internal resistance r: V = ε − Ir while discharging, V = ε + Ir while being charged. emf = terminal p.d. on open circuit.
  • I = ε/(R + r). Power to R is maximum when R = r. Two different loads R1, R2 get equal power when r = √(R1R2).
  • Series cells: ε adds, r adds. Parallel cells: εeq = (ε1/r1 + ε2/r2)/(1/r1 + 1/r2), 1/req = 1/r1 + 1/r2.
  • Kirchhoff: junction rule (charge conservation), loop rule (energy conservation).
  • P = VI = I2R = V2/R. A bulb rated P0, V0 has R = V02/P0; on V it uses P0(V/V0)2. In series the lower-wattage bulb (higher R) glows brighter. 1 unit = 1 kWh = 3.6 × 106 J.

DerivationDrift velocity, I = neAvd and resistivity

Free electrons, n per m3, mean time between collisions τ.

  1. Acceleration a = eE/m; after a collision the velocity is random, so the average gained before the next is vd = aτ = eEτ/m.

  3. Substitute vd: I = (ne2τ/m)AE. With E = V/l: V = (m/ne2τ)(l/A) I = ρl/A · I.

DerivationWheatstone bridge balance

A null method: the reading is taken when no current flows through the galvanometer.

  1. At balance Ig = 0, so P and Q carry the same current I1, R and S the same I2, and VB = VD.

Loop radius R, current I, point P on the axis at distance x from the centre.

  4. B = (μ0/4π)(I/r2)(R/r)(2πR) = μ0IR2/2(R2 + x2)3/2; with N turns multiply by N.

DerivationAmpère’s law: the long straight wire and the solenoid

Choose an Ampèrian loop along which B is constant and tangential, or perpendicular (no contribution).

  1. Straight wire: a circle of radius r round the wire. By symmetry B is tangential and the same all round: B(2πr) = μ0I ⇒ B = μ0I/2πr.
  2. Solenoid: a rectangle of length h with one side inside along the axis and one outside. The outside side has B ≈ 0 and the two ends are perpendicular to B.

  4. Charge in B (the same symmetry idea): qvB = mv2/r gives r = mv/qB and T = 2πr/v = 2πm/qB, the same for every speed.

ConceptBar magnet and Gauss’s law for magnetism

  • A bar magnet is a magnetic dipole of moment m, pointing from its S pole to its N pole. Its field lines are closed loops (inside: S to N), so ∮B·dS = 0: there are no isolated poles.
  • In a uniform B: τ = mB sinθ, U = −mB cosθ; work to turn from θ1 to θ2 = mB(cosθ1 − cosθ2), 2mB from aligned to reversed. Small oscillations: T = 2π√(I/mB).
  • Field of a short magnet: axial 2(μ0/4π)m/r3 along m, equatorial (μ0/4π)m/r3 opposite to m: the same forms as the electric dipole with p → m and 1/4πε0 → μ0/4π.
  • Equivalent solenoid: a bar magnet and a current-carrying solenoid of the same moment (m = NIA) have the same external field: each loop of the solenoid is a tiny dipole, and their fields add to the bar-magnet pattern.

ConceptMagnetic materials

  • H = B/μ0 − M; M = χH; B = μ0(1 + χ)H = μH; μr = 1 + χ.
  • Diamagnetic (Bi, Cu, water, N2): χ small and negative, μr slightly < 1; weakly repelled, move from strong to weak field; atoms have no net moment; χ independent of temperature. A superconductor is a perfect diamagnet: χ = −1, μr = 0, B = 0 inside (Meissner effect).
  • Paramagnetic (Al, Na, O2): χ small and positive; weakly attracted; atoms have permanent moments. Curie law χ = C/T: cooling increases χ.
  • Ferromagnetic (Fe, Co, Ni, gadolinium): χ very large (103–105), strongly attracted; atomic moments line up in domains, and an applied field grows the aligned domains. Above the Curie temperature TC it becomes paramagnetic, with χ = C/(T − TC).
  • Effect of temperature (the syllabus line): diamagnetism is unaffected; paramagnetic χ falls as 1/T; ferromagnetism is lost above TC.
  • A ferromagnetic core inside a solenoid multiplies B by μr (hundreds to thousands).

ConceptFaraday, Lenz and motional emf

  • Flux Φ = BA cosθ (scalar, weber = T m2). Faraday: ε = −N dΦ/dt; an emf appears whenever the flux linked changes, by changing B, A or the angle.
  • Lenz: the induced current opposes the change that causes it (energy conservation). A magnet approaching a coil is repelled; one leaving is attracted. A magnet rotated about its own axis, coaxial with a coil, changes no flux: no current.
  • Induced charge q = NΔΦ/R: depends on the change of flux, not on how fast.
  • Motional emf ε = Blv for a rod moving perpendicular to B. A rod of length l rotating about one end: ε = ½Bωl2. An aircraft wing (span l) cuts the vertical component: ε = BVlv.
  • Pulling a rod at steady speed needs force B2l2v/R; the mechanical power equals the I2R heat.
  • A coil rotating in B (generator): ε = NBAω sinωt, peak NBAω: more turns, larger area, stronger field or faster rotation all raise it.

Conceptrms values and the three elements

  • v = V0 sinωt. rms values: Vrms = V0/√2, Irms = I0/√2; meters read rms. Mains 220 V is rms, peak 311 V. Average over a full cycle is zero; over a half cycle 2I0/π. For i = i1 cosωt + i2 sinωt, Irms = √((i12 + i22)/2).
  • R: v and i in phase. L: XL = ωL, current lags by 90°. C: XC = 1/ωC, current leads by 90°. A capacitor blocks d.c. (XC → ∞ at ω = 0); an inductor passes d.c. and blocks high frequency.
  • Pure L or pure C: average power zero (wattless current). A coil with resistance: on d.c. it shows only R; on a.c. Z = √(R2 + XL2).

ConceptSeries LCR, resonance, power, transformer

  • Z = √(R2 + (XL − XC)2), tanφ = (XL − XC)/R. Voltages across L and C are 180° apart, so V = √(VR2 + (VL − VC)2), not the arithmetic sum.
  • Resonance XL = XC: ω0 = 1/√LC, Z = R (minimum), current maximum, in phase with V, power factor 1. VL and VC can each exceed the supply voltage. Q-factor = ω0L/R = (1/R)√(L/C); sharpness ∝ Q, so a small R gives better tuning.
  • Average power P = VrmsIrms cosφ, cosφ = R/Z. A choke coil (large L, small R) controls current with almost no power loss, unlike a resistor.
  • Transformer (a.c. only): Vs/Vp = Ns/Np = Ip/Is (ideal, efficiency 100%). Step-down raises the current. A real transformer loses a little energy (resistance of the windings, heating of the core, flux leakage), so its efficiency is just under 100%. AC generator: a coil of N turns, area A turning at ω in B gives ε = NBAω sinωt.

ConceptDisplacement current and EM waves

  • Maxwell added the displacement current id = ε0 dΦE/dt to Ampère’s law: ∮B·dl = μ0(ic + id). A changing electric field makes a magnetic field. Between the plates of a charging capacitor id equals the conduction current in the wires.

  • Speed c = 1/√(μ0ε0) = 3 × 108 m/s in vacuum for every wavelength; in a medium v = 1/√(με) = c/√(μrεr), so n = √(μrεr).

ConceptMirrors and lenses

  • Cartesian sign convention: distances from the pole/optical centre, positive in the direction of incident light (to the right), heights positive upwards. Concave mirror and concave lens have f < 0; convex mirror and convex lens f > 0.
  • Mirror 1/v + 1/u = 1/f, f = R/2, m = −v/u. Lens 1/v − 1/u = 1/f, m = v/u. Real images are inverted (m < 0); virtual images are erect (m > 0).
  • A concave mirror gives m = +1.5 (erect, magnified) only when the object is between F and P. A convex mirror and a concave lens always give virtual, erect, diminished images.
  • Covering half a lens leaves the whole image but halves its brightness.
  • Power P = 1/f (f in metres), in dioptres; lenses in contact: P = P1 + P2.
  • Lens maker: 1/f = (n21 − 1)(1/R1 − 1/R2), n21 = nlens/nmedium. A glass lens in water has a longer focal length (×4 for n = 1.5 in n = 4/3); in a liquid of the same index it vanishes (f → ∞); in a denser liquid a convex lens diverges. An equiconvex lens cut along its axis keeps f; cut across (into two plano-convex halves), each has 2f.

ConceptRefraction, TIR, prism and dispersion

  • Snell: n1 sin i = n2 sin r; n = c/v. Frequency does not change on refraction; speed and wavelength drop by n. n21 = n2/n1, n12 = 1/n21.
  • Apparent depth = real depth/n. Time to cross a thickness t: nt/c.
  • Refraction at one spherical surface: n2/v − n1/u = (n2 − n1)/R.
  • TIR: from denser to rarer, i > c, sin c = 1/n (n of denser relative to rarer). Larger n ⇒ smaller c (diamond 24°). Uses: optical fibres (core denser than cladding), totally reflecting prisms, sparkle of diamond, mirage. Apparent depth is not a TIR effect.
  • Prism: r1 + r2 = A, δ = i + e − A. At minimum deviation i = e, r = A/2 and n = sin((A + δm)/2)/sin(A/2). Thin prism δ = (n − 1)A.
  • Dispersion: n is larger for violet, so violet deviates most (δ = (n − 1)A for a thin prism) and fviolet < fred for a lens. In a medium red travels fastest and violet slowest; in vacuum all colours travel at c.

ConceptOptical instruments

  • Simple microscope: m = 1 + D/f (image at D = 25 cm), m = D/f (image at infinity).

ConceptWavefronts and interference

  • A wavefront is a surface of constant phase; rays are normal to it. Point source: spherical; line source: cylindrical; distant source: plane. A convex lens turns a plane wavefront into a converging spherical one.
  • Huygens: every point on a wavefront is a source of secondary wavelets; the new wavefront is their forward envelope. It gives the laws of reflection and refraction, and shows light is slower in a denser medium.
  • Coherent sources keep a constant phase difference: obtained by splitting one source (YDSE slits). Two independent bulbs are incoherent.
  • Resultant intensity I = I1 + I2 + 2√(I1I2) cosφ; for equal sources I = 4I0 cos2(φ/2). Imax/Imin = ((√I1 + √I2)/(√I1 − √I2))2. Phase difference = (2π/λ) × path difference.
  • YDSE: bright at path difference nλ, dark at (n + ½)λ; fringe width β = λD/d, angular width λ/d. In a liquid β falls by n. Cover one slit: the interference fringes vanish and only single-slit diffraction remains. White light: central white fringe, coloured fringes either side.

ConceptSingle-slit diffraction

  • Single slit of width a: minima at a sinθ = nλ; central maximum is twice as wide (angular width 2λ/a, linear 2λD/a) and much brighter than the rest. Narrower slit ⇒ wider pattern. Diffraction is noticeable when the obstacle is comparable to λ.
  • Why the edges of the central maximum are dark at a sinθ = λ: split the slit into two halves; each point in the top half has a partner in the bottom half whose path differs by λ/2, so the pairs cancel.
  • Secondary maxima lie near a sinθ = (n + ½)λ and fade fast (about 1/22 and 1/62 of the centre).
  • In a liquid of index n, λ and so the width of the central maximum shrink by n.
  • Interference versus diffraction: YDSE fringes are equally wide and (for equal slits) equally bright; diffraction fringes are not.

ConceptPhotoelectric effect

  • Light of frequency above the threshold ν0 ejects electrons instantly, however dim. Below ν0: none, however bright.
  • Intensity (number of photons per second) fixes the photocurrent and the saturation current; it does not change Kmax or the stopping potential.
  • Frequency fixes Kmax = hν − φ = eV0; it does not change the saturation current (at the same intensity).
  • V0–ν graph: a straight line of slope h/e for every metal, ν-intercept ν0, extended V-intercept −φ/e.
  • Moving a point source to twice the distance cuts the intensity to a quarter (inverse square), so the current drops to a quarter; V0 stays the same.
  • Wave theory cannot explain the threshold, the instant emission or the independence of Kmax from intensity: these show the particle nature of light (so do the Compton effect and pair production). Interference, diffraction and polarisation show its wave nature.
  • Photon: E = hν = hc/λ = 1240/λ(nm) eV; p = h/λ = E/c; rest mass zero; speed c; energy depends on frequency only. Contributors: Hertz, Hallwachs, Lenard, Einstein, Millikan.

ConceptMatter waves

  • de Broglie: λ = h/p = h/mv = h/√(2mK) = h/√(2mqV).
  • Electron through V volts: λ = 1.227/√V nm (100 V ⇒ 0.123 nm, X-ray sized; hence electron diffraction, Davisson–Germer).
  • Same momentum ⇒ same λ (electron and photon too). Same KE ⇒ λ ∝ 1/√m: electron longest, then proton, neutron, deuteron, α shortest. Same accelerating V ⇒ λ ∝ 1/√(mq).
  • Macroscopic bodies have absurdly small λ (a 150 g ball at 30 m/s: 1.5 × 10−34 m), which is why we never see their wave nature.
  • A charged particle in a uniform B keeps its speed, so its de Broglie wavelength does not change.

DerivationEinstein’s photoelectric equation

Energy bookkeeping for one photon and one electron.

  1. A photon gives all its energy hν to one electron. At least φ (the work function) is spent getting out of the surface.

ConceptHydrogen line spectra (qualitative)

  • Each line is a photon from a jump between two Bohr levels: hν = Ei − Ef, λ(nm) = 1240/ΔE(eV). Only these discrete energies are possible, so the spectrum is a set of lines, not a continuum.

  • An atom in level n can drop in many ways: a sample excited to n emits n(n − 1)/2 different lines (a single atom at most n − 1). Absorption from the ground state gives only Lyman lines.

ConceptThe nucleus

  • Nuclide AZX: Z protons, N = A − Z neutrons. Isotopes: same Z (12C, 14C). Isobars: same A (3H, 3He). Isotones: same N.
  • R = R0A1/3, R0 = 1.2 fm. Volume ∝ A, so the density is the same for all nuclei (about 2.3 × 1017 kg/m3).
  • Mass defect Δm = Zmp + (A − Z)mn − M; binding energy BE = Δm c2, with 1 u = 931.5 MeV/c2.
  • Nuclear force: strongest of all, short range (a few fm), charge independent (p–p, n–n, n–p alike), saturates, repulsive below about 0.8 fm.
  • BE/A is about 8 MeV for most nuclei, peaks near A = 56 (Fe, 8.8 MeV). Fission of heavy nuclei and fusion of light ones both move towards the peak and release energy. 4He has an unusually high BE/A (7.07 MeV).
  • Fission chain reaction: each fission releases more than one neutron (2–3). Reactor: moderator (heavy water, graphite) slows neutrons to thermal speeds; control rods (cadmium, boron) absorb them; coolant removes heat. Fusion powers the Sun (hydrogen to helium) and needs very high temperature.

DerivationNuclear radius and density

Nucleons packed like incompressible balls: volume proportional to the number of nucleons.

  1. Volume ∝ A ⇒ (4/3)πR3 ∝ A ⇒ R = R0A1/3, R0 ≈ 1.2 fm (from α/electron scattering).
  2. Mass ≈ A mu (mu = 1.66 × 10−27 kg), so ρ = A mu/((4/3)πR03A) = 3mu/4πR03.
  3. ρ = 3(1.66 × 10−27)/(4π(1.2 × 10−15)3) ≈ 2.3 × 1017 kg/m3: A cancels, so every nucleus has the same density.
  4. Ratios follow at once: R1/R2 = (A1/A2)1/3, volumes ∝ A, densities 1 : 1.

ConceptBands and doping

  • Conductor: bands overlap (Eg ≈ 0). Semiconductor: small gap (Si 1.1 eV, Ge 0.7 eV). Insulator: large gap (> 3 eV, diamond 5.4 eV).
  • Intrinsic: ne = nh = ni; both electrons and holes conduct. Heating raises the number of carriers, so resistance falls with temperature (a metal’s rises). Cooled to 0 K a pure semiconductor is an insulator.
  • n-type: pentavalent donor (P, As, Sb); electrons majority. Donor level just below the conduction band. p-type: trivalent acceptor (B, Al, In, Ga); holes majority; acceptor level just above the valence band. Both remain electrically neutral.
  • Mass-action law nenh = ni2: adding donors raises ne and lowers nh.
  • Light can create an electron–hole pair only if hν ≥ Eg: λmax = 1240/Eg(eV) nm.

ConceptThe p–n junction and rectifiers

  • Diffusion of majority carriers leaves a depletion layer of immobile ions, with no free carriers (hence high resistance), and a barrier field from n to p. In equilibrium diffusion and drift currents balance: no net current.
  • Forward bias (p to +): barrier lowered, depletion layer narrower, large current after the knee (0.7 V Si, 0.3 V Ge). Reverse bias: barrier raised, depletion layer wider, tiny minority-carrier current (μA) until breakdown. A diode is forward biased whenever the p-side is at the higher potential, e.g. p at 0 V and n at −4 V.
  • Half-wave rectifier: one diode, output on alternate half-cycles, output frequency = input frequency. Full-wave (centre-tap or bridge): output on both halves, frequency doubled (50 Hz → 100 Hz). A capacitor across the load filters the ripple; the capacitor charges to the peak voltage.
