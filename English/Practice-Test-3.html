Question 1Reading Comprehension: Factual Passages
Passage 1: Water Temples of the Dry West (Q1–9)

For centuries the people of western India’s dry belt lived with a simple problem: the rain came in a few weeks of monsoon and then vanished for the rest of the year. Their answer was the stepwell, called a vav in Gujarat and a baori or bawdi in Rajasthan. Instead of lowering a bucket into a narrow shaft, a person walked down flights of stone steps to reach water that sank lower as the dry months went by. The stone kept the water cool and shaded, and the steps kept it within reach at every level.

Stepwells were never merely utilitarian. Many were paid for by queens and wealthy merchants as acts of charity, and their builders treated them as sacred spaces. The most celebrated, Rani ki Vav at Patan in Gujarat, was built in the eleventh century, by tradition at the command of a queen in memory of her husband. Its seven storeys of galleries carry hundreds of carved figures, many of them forms of Vishnu, so that a visitor going down to the water passes through what is, in effect, a temple turned upside down. Floods later filled it with silt and it lay buried for centuries – an accident that protected its sculptures from weather and from vandals. Archaeologists cleared it in the late twentieth century, and UNESCO named it a World Heritage Site in 2014.

Stepwells were social places too. Women met there to draw water and exchange news, travellers rested in the cool galleries, and on the hottest afternoons the lowest landings could be several degrees cooler than the ground above.

Their decline began in the colonial period. Piped water and hand pumps made them look obsolete, and some officials sealed them as unhygienic. Many became rubbish dumps, and as the groundwater table fell, most simply ran dry. Of the thousands that once existed, only a fraction still hold water.

Interest has revived in recent years. A few towns have cleared their stepwells and reconnected them to the rainwater channels that once fed them, and conservationists argue that such structures could again help recharge the ground below. The point, they say, is not to be sentimental about the past but to admit that the old builders understood something modern planners forgot: in a dry land, water must be caught where it falls and stored where the sun cannot steal it.

According to the passage, why did a stepwell need flights of steps rather than a single narrow shaft?
  1. Steps made the structure strong enough to survive floods.
  2. The water level fell through the dry months, and steps kept it within reach at every level.
  3. Steps allowed animals to be led down to drink.
  4. Rulers wanted visitors to climb slowly as an act of worship.
Concept to recallDirect-fact question: find the sentence that explains the design (“water that sank lower … the steps kept it within reach at every level”).
  1. Paragraph 1: a person walked down to reach water “that sank lower as the dry months went by”.
  2. So the steps let people follow a falling water level.
  3. Floods are mentioned only for Rani ki Vav’s burial; animals are never mentioned; worship explains the carvings, not the steps.

Answer: option (2) — The water level fell through the dry months, and steps kept it within reach at every level.

TrapThe sacred character of stepwells is real, but it explains the decoration, not why there are steps.

Question 10Reading Comprehension: Narrative and Literary Passages
Passage 2: The Sapling (Q10–16)

The monsoon had softened the ground behind the house, and Aaji had been waiting for it. She knelt by the hole she had dug that morning, her sari tucked up at the knees, while Meenal held the sapling – a thin stick with five glossy leaves – as carefully as she had once held her baby cousin.

Mr Kulkarni from next door leaned over the wall. “A mango tree, Aaji? At your age? It will be eight years before it gives a single fruit.”

“Seven, if the rains are kind,” said Aaji, without looking up. “Pass me the trowel, Meenu.”

“And who is going to eat them?” he went on, laughing. “Not you, I think.”

Aaji pressed the earth down round the stem with the flat of her palm. “When I was your daughter’s age,” she said, “I climbed a mango tree in my grandfather’s village every summer. I never once asked who had planted it.” She sat back on her heels. “Somebody I never met fed me for twenty summers. It is time I paid the debt.”

Mr Kulkarni opened his mouth, closed it, and went indoors.

Meenal frowned at the sapling. “But Aaji, he is right. You won’t –”

“Then you will,” said Aaji briskly. “And you will not thank me, because you will not remember a time when it was not there. That is how it should be.”

For the rest of the monsoon that corner of the garden was given over to the sapling. Meenal built a ring of bricks round it to keep the goats away, and twice, when a storm flattened it, she and Aaji tied it back to its stake with strips of an old sari.

The next spring, on a Sunday morning, Meenal looked over the wall and saw Mr Kulkarni kneeling in his own garden beside a freshly dug hole, a small sapling in his hands. He saw her watching and became very busy with his spade.

Meenal held the sapling “as carefully as she had once held her baby cousin”. This suggests that she
  1. was afraid of being scolded by Aaji
  2. found the sapling too heavy to hold
  3. did not want Mr Kulkarni to see it
  4. treated it as something fragile and precious
Concept to recallInference from a comparison: a baby is held gently and protectively.
  1. A baby is held with great care because it is delicate.
  2. So Meenal handles the thin sapling as something fragile that matters.
  3. Nothing suggests fear of a scolding, weight, or hiding it from the neighbour.

Answer: option (4) — treated it as something fragile and precious
