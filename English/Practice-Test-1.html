Question 1Reading Comprehension: Factual Passages
Passage I — read it and answer Q1–8

Walk past an old stone wall or the bark of a roadside tree and you will probably notice patches of grey-green, orange or yellow crust that look like flaking paint. Most people dismiss them as dirt or mould. They are in fact lichens, and they are among the most curious partnerships in the living world. A lichen is not a single organism but a composite: a fungus provides the structure and shelter, while an alga or a cyanobacterium living within its threads makes food through photosynthesis. Neither partner, on its own, could survive on bare rock; together they colonise surfaces from desert boulders to Antarctic cliffs.

What makes lichens valuable to scientists is precisely what makes them vulnerable. Having no roots and no waxy protective skin, they absorb water and nutrients directly from rain, mist and the air itself. Along with moisture, they soak up whatever the air carries — including sulphur dioxide, nitrogen compounds and heavy metals. Because they cannot shed leaves or excrete these substances, the pollutants accumulate in their tissues. Sensitive species, especially the bushy and leafy kinds, wither and vanish when the air turns foul, while a few hardy crust-forming species persist.

This uneven tolerance turns lichens into living instruments. By recording which species grow on trees across a city, researchers can draw maps of air quality at a fraction of the cost of electronic monitors. In several European cities in the twentieth century, a ‘lichen desert’ was observed around industrial centres, where almost nothing grew. When clean-air laws reduced sulphur emissions, lichens gradually returned — a recovery that confirmed what the instruments had measured.

Yet lichens are not perfect gauges. They grow extremely slowly, sometimes only a millimetre a year, so they reflect conditions over years rather than days; a sudden spike of smoke will not register at once. Their response also depends on bark acidity, shade and humidity, which must be accounted for. Used carefully, however, they offer something machines cannot: a record of how the air has treated living things over time. In that sense, the humble crust on the wall is less a stain than a diary.

According to the passage, a lichen is best described as
  1. a partnership between a fungus and an organism that photosynthesises
  2. a kind of mould that grows on damp stone and bark
  3. a fungus that feeds on the decaying bark of trees
  4. a single plant without roots or leaves
Concept to recallDefinition questions: find the sentence that says “X is …” and match its key idea, not the words of the popular misconception the passage corrects.
  1. Paragraph 1: “A lichen is not a single organism but a composite: a fungus … while an alga or a cyanobacterium … makes food through photosynthesis.”
  2. So it is a partnership of a fungus and a photosynthesising partner.
  3. The “mould” answer is what “most people” wrongly think; the passage raises that view only to correct it. “A single plant” contradicts “not a single organism”.

Answer: option (1) — a partnership between a fungus and an organism that photosynthesises

TrapPicking the mistaken popular view (“dirt or mould”) because those words appear in the passage.

(B) Bushy and leafy lichens are the most tolerant of polluted air.

Question 9Reading Comprehension: Narrative and Literary Passages
Passage II — read it and answer Q9–13

Every evening at six, Mr Dhar pulled down the shutter of his narrow shop on Bakery Lane, where he had mended watches and wall clocks for thirty-one years. The lane had changed around him. The bakery that gave it its name had become a phone shop, and the boys who once pressed their noses against his window to watch the tiny wheels turning now walked past with their eyes on their screens.

So he was surprised one wet afternoon when a girl of about eleven set a shoebox on his counter. Inside lay an old transistor radio, its case cracked, its dial frozen at a station that no longer existed.

“It was my grandfather’s,” she said. “Nobody fixes these any more. The man at the phone shop laughed.”

Mr Dhar did not laugh. He turned the radio over in his stained fingers the way a doctor might lift a sparrow. “I mend clocks,” he said, “not radios.”

“Clocks have insides too,” she replied, and for some reason he could not argue.

She came back every day after school. He showed her how to loosen a screw without stripping its head, and why one must never force a part that does not want to move. She showed him how to look up a circuit diagram on her mother’s phone. Between them, slowly, the radio gave up its secrets: a corroded battery clip, a loose wire, a dial cord that had snapped.

On the ninth day, he turned the knob. For a moment there was only a dry hiss, like rain on a tin roof. Then, faintly, a voice rose out of the static, reading the evening news.

The girl did not cheer. She simply sat very still, listening, as though someone she had lost were speaking from the next room.

That evening Mr Dhar pulled down his shutter later than usual. He found, to his surprise, that he was in no hurry to go home.

Why was Mr Dhar surprised when the girl came into his shop?
  1. Young people of the lane had long stopped taking an interest in his work.
  2. He had never seen a transistor radio before.
  3. She was the first customer he had had in thirty-one years.
  4. The phone shop usually sent its customers to him.
Concept to recallNarrative inference: “So he was surprised” points back to the paragraph before it — the reason lies there.
  1. Paragraph 1: the boys who once watched the wheels “now walked past with their eyes on their screens”.
  2. “So he was surprised” — a child coming in breaks that pattern.
  3. Nothing says he had no customers at all for thirty-one years (that is how long he had worked), and the phone shop “laughed”, it did not refer her.

Question 39Fill in the Blanks and Cloze

  3. no article
