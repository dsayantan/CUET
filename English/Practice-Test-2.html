Question 1Reading Comprehension: Factual Passages
Passage I: The Case for Friction (Q1–8)

[1] Every few years a new technology arrives promising to make learning effortless. The radio was going to bring the finest lecturers into every village; television was going to replace the dull textbook; now software that answers any question in seconds is offered as the long-awaited private tutor for every child. Each promise contains a truth, and each has been followed, a decade later, by the quiet discovery that test scores have barely moved. It is worth asking why.

[2] Part of the answer is that effort is not an unfortunate cost of learning; it is much of the learning itself. When a student struggles to recall a formula, the struggle strengthens the very memory she is reaching for. When she writes a muddled paragraph and has to untangle it, she discovers what she actually thinks. Psychologists have a name for this, “desirable difficulty”, and the adjective matters. Not every obstacle helps; a textbook printed in a smudged typeface teaches nothing but irritation. But the right kind of difficulty, the kind that forces the mind to retrieve, compare and rebuild, is not a defect to be engineered away.

[3] The companies that build educational software, however, are judged by a different measure. Their products succeed when they are used, and people use what feels easy. An app that makes a learner feel clever will be opened again tomorrow; an app that makes her feel slow, even if it is teaching her more, will be deleted. The market therefore rewards the sensation of progress rather than progress itself, and it would be naive to expect firms to resist that reward out of conscience alone.

[4] None of this is an argument for banning the new tools, which would be both futile and foolish. A calculator did not destroy arithmetic; it moved the effort from long division to the harder question of which calculation to perform. The danger lies not in the machine but in a habit of mind that treats every hesitation as a failure of design. Schools that hand students a tool capable of writing their essays and then grade only the finished essay should not be surprised when the essays improve and the writers do not.

[5] What is needed, then, is not less technology but a clearer sense of what it is for. Teachers might grade the drafts as well as the final version, ask students to explain aloud what the software produced, or set problems that the tool can assist with but not complete. Such measures cost time, and time is precisely what overstretched schools lack. Yet a system that saves an hour of the student’s effort at the price of the understanding that effort would have bought has not saved anything at all. It has merely moved the bill to a later date, when it will be harder to pay.

(An original passage written for this practice test.)

Which of the following best states the central argument of the passage?
  1. Educational technology has always failed and its use in schools should be restricted.
  2. Software companies deliberately design their products to harm learners.
  3. The right kind of effort is part of learning itself, so technology that removes every difficulty can undermine the learning it claims to improve.
  4. Schools should replace written essays with oral examinations.
Concept to recallThe central argument is the claim the whole passage defends. It is not a detail, and it is not something the passage says only in passing.
  1. Paragraph 2 sets out the thesis: effort “is much of the learning itself”. Paragraphs 3–5 show how tools and markets push against that effort, and what schools can do about it.
  2. The “restricted” option is wrong because paragraph 4 explicitly rejects banning the tools as “futile and foolish”.
  3. The other two options are wrong as well. The passage never says firms deliberately harm learners; they respond to a market. Oral explanation is only one of several suggestions.

Answer: option (3) — The right kind of effort is part of learning itself, so technology that removes every difficulty can undermine the learning it claims to improve.

TrapThe “restrict technology” option sounds like the passage’s mood, but the author says plainly that the answer is “not less technology”.

Question 9Reading Comprehension: Narrative and Literary Passages
Passage II: The Ledger (Q9–12)

My grandmother ran a lending library from the front room of her house, though she would have laughed at so grand a name for two almirahs and a wooden bench. The books had come to her one at a time, over forty years: from neighbours who were moving away, from a schoolmaster who had died without heirs, from a rag-and-bone man who sold her a carton of water-stained novels for less than the price of a kilo of onions. She charged nothing. She asked only that borrowers write their names in a long cloth-bound ledger, and that they bring the book back “in the condition in which it set out”, a phrase she pronounced as though the books were travellers who might come home sunburnt.

I was nine the summer I was made her clerk. My duties were to blot the ledger, to sort the returns, and to say, as gravely as I could manage, “Fourteen days, please,” to grown men twice my height. I thought the job beneath me. I wanted to be reading, not recording who else was reading, and I made my feelings known in the way children do, by doing everything slowly.

She never scolded me for it. Instead, one afternoon when the room was empty, she opened the ledger at its first page and ran her finger down the columns. Here was the postman who had borrowed every book on birds, one after another, for a whole winter. Here was a girl who had taken the same book of poems eleven times and then, abruptly, never came again. “She got married and moved to Nagpur,” my grandmother said. “I sent the book after her. It was hers more than mine by then.” Here, in faded ink, was a name I recognised with a jolt: my own mother’s, at about my age, borrowing a book whose title I had never heard her mention.

I understood then (or I understand now, which in memory amounts to the same thing) that the ledger was not a record of books at all. It was a record of people at the moments when they had wanted something they could not afford, and had been given it without being made to feel poor. My grandmother had kept the accounts of a whole street’s longing.

The almirahs went to a school when she died, and the ledger came to me. I have not added a name to it. I take it out sometimes and read it the way other people read novels, which is to say, for the parts that are not written down.
