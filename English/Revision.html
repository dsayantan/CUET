  2. Read the passage once at speed. Pencil one word beside each paragraph: problem, method, numbers, limits.

Hedge words in the passage — may, some, often, about, tends to — are why an option with all or always is usually wrong.

ConceptNOT / EXCEPT / incorrect stems, and the (A)–(E) sets

These are the commonest single stem in factual RC. Read the stem twice and write NOT in the margin; under time pressure students answer the opposite question.

  1. Mark each option T (supported), F (contradicted) or ? (not mentioned).
  2. For “which is NOT true / not mentioned” the answer is the F or the ?. The three Ts are all in the passage, often in one paragraph — find that paragraph and tick them off together.
  3. For an (A)–(E) set, settle the statement you are surest of first, then strike every option set that disagrees with it. Usually two checks leave one option.

ConceptPractice passage A

Practice passage A — factual (written for this page)

Most cities in India receive enough rain in four monsoon months to meet their water needs for the whole year, yet many of them buy water from tankers by April. The rain is not missing; it is running away. Concrete roofs and roads send it into drains within minutes, and the drains carry it out of the city.

Rooftop harvesting reverses this. A pipe leads water from the roof through a simple sand-and-gravel filter into a tank or a recharge pit. A 100-square-metre roof in a city with 800 mm of rain can collect about 80,000 litres a year — several years’ drinking and cooking water for a family of four. Some municipalities now refuse building permission without such a system.

The method is not a cure-all. Tanks must be cleaned before each monsoon, and in crowded areas the first rain washes dust and bird droppings off the roof, so the first flush is best thrown away. Still, it asks for little more than a pipe, a filter and a habit.

Worked exampleNOT stem on passage A

Which of the following is NOT stated in passage A?

(1) City drains carry rain-water out of the city.
(2) A filter of sand and gravel is used before storage.
(3) Rooftop harvesting is the cheapest source of water in a city.
(4) The first rain off a roof is best discarded.
  1. Option 1: paragraph 1, “the drains carry it out of the city” — stated.
  2. Option 2: paragraph 2, “sand-and-gravel filter” — stated.
  3. Option 4: paragraph 3, “the first flush is best thrown away” — stated.
  4. Option 3: the passage says it needs “little more than a pipe, a filter and a habit” but never compares its cost with other sources. “Cheapest” is an over-reach.

Answer: (3) Rooftop harvesting is the cheapest source of water in a city.

Worked exampleInference on passage A

It can be inferred from passage A that the cities’ water shortage is mainly a problem of

(1) too little rainfall
(2) failing to keep the rain that falls
(3) dirty roofs
(4) tankers charging too much
  1. Paragraph 1: “The rain is not missing; it is running away.”
  2. So the shortage is about capturing water, not about rainfall — one step from the text.
  3. Dirty roofs are a limitation of the method, not the cause of the shortage; tanker prices are never discussed.

Answer: (2) failing to keep the rain that falls

Worked exampleTitle for passage A

The most suitable title for passage A is

(1) The Monsoon in India
(2) Cleaning Water Tanks
(3) Catching the Rain Before It Leaves
(4) Why Tankers Are Expensive
  1. The passage moves problem (rain runs off) → solution (rooftop harvesting) → limits.
  2. “The Monsoon in India” is too broad; “Cleaning Water Tanks” covers one sentence; tanker prices are never discussed.
  3. Only option 3 spans the whole passage in its own angle.

Answer: (3) Catching the Rain Before It Leaves

Worked exampleWord in context on passage A

As used in passage A, “a cure-all” means

(1) a medicine
(2) a complete answer to every difficulty
(3) a costly method
(4) a new invention
  1. The next sentence lists the method’s drawbacks, so “not a cure-all” = it does not solve everything.
  2. The literal “medicine” sense is the trap.

Answer: (2) a complete answer to every difficulty

TrapsTraps in factual RC

  • Answering from what you know about the topic instead of what the passage says.
  • Missing the NOT in “Which of the following is not …” — the commonest lost mark.
  • Choosing the option that repeats the passage’s exact words but changes the relation (cause/effect, more/less).
  • Numbers: check units and which quantity the figure belongs to; distractors swap two figures from the same paragraph.
  • A title that names only the first paragraph’s example.
  • In (A)–(E) sets, ticking a statement that is half right; one false half makes the statement false.

ConceptReading a story or memoir

TrackAsk yourself
Whothe narrator (I) or a third person? whose feelings are we told?
What changesa story is a change: of place, mind, fortune. Name it in one line.
Motivewhy a character acts: the text usually gives the reason one sentence before or after the act.
Attitudehow the narrator feels about events: fond, amused, regretful, bitter (use the tone spectrum).
Lessonthe central idea is the change plus the narrator’s attitude to it.

Literary questions reward the implied meaning, but the implication must still grow out of a specific line. A figurative line is never asking about its literal picture.

ConceptReading a poem in 90 seconds

  1. Read it once for the situation: who speaks, to whom, where, what moment.
  2. Find the turn: a but, now, yet, or a change of tense, usually splits the poem in two (then/now, hope/loss).
  3. For “what does X suggest”, ask what the image does in the poem, not what the object is.
  4. Name devices by the test in the Figures-of-Speech chapter; a poem question often hides a device question.
  5. Tone: the closing lines usually decide it.

DiagramTone of a literary passage

The same spectrum serves; literary tones sit further out to the edges than factual ones. Nostalgic, wistful, ironic, satirical and humorous are the literary favourites.

ConceptPractice texts B and C

Practice passage B — narrative (written for this page)

Every evening Meera carried the kettle along platform two while her father poured tea at the stall. That Tuesday a passenger in a hurry left a book on the bench, a thick one with a cracked green cover. Meera ran after the moving train, the book held above her head like a lamp, until the last carriage slid past the end of the platform.

She stood there a long time, out of breath. Her father said she could sell it to the paper-man for twelve rupees. She did not. She read it instead, a page each night under the stall’s single bulb, and by the time the monsoon came she had reached the last chapter. She kept the book on the shelf beside the sugar tin, in case the man ever came back on the same train.

Practice poem C (written for this page)

The old well in our courtyard
keeps the sky at the bottom of its throat;
each morning grandmother lowers a bucket
and pulls up a trembling coin of light.
Now a tap stands where the rope once sang,
quick, and cold, and certain.
The well says nothing. It has learned
how to keep water, and how to wait.

Worked exampleMotive (passage B)

Why did Meera run after the train?

(1) She wanted the twelve rupees as a reward.
(2) She wanted to return the book to its owner.
(3) Her father had told her to.
(4) She was late with the kettle.
  1. She runs “with the book held above her head” after the passenger who left it: she means to give it back.
  2. The twelve rupees appear later, and she refuses them; the father suggests selling, not chasing.

Answer: (2) She wanted to return the book to its owner.

Worked exampleNOT stem (passage B)

Which of the following does NOT hold true for Meera?

(1) She was honest.
(2) She was patient.
(3) She sold the book to the paper-man.
(4) She hoped the owner might return.
  1. Honest: she chases the owner. Patient: a page a night until the monsoon. Hopeful: keeps it “in case the man ever came back”.
  2. “She did not” sell it — option 3 contradicts the passage.

Answer: (3) She sold the book to the paper-man.

Worked exampleWhat a line suggests (poem C)

In poem C, “a trembling coin of light” refers to

(1) a coin dropped into the well
(2) the sky reflected in the water in the bucket
(3) the grandmother’s savings
(4) a lamp kept by the well
  1. Line 2: the well “keeps the sky at the bottom”; the bucket brings up that water.
  2. A small round bright reflection that shakes as the bucket rises = a “trembling coin of light” (a metaphor).
  3. Option 1 takes “coin” literally — the classic trap.

Answer: (2) the sky reflected in the water in the bucket

Worked exampleDevice and tone (poem C)

The lines “The well says nothing. It has learned / how to keep water, and how to wait” use ______ and the tone of the poem is ______.

(1) simile; angry
(2) personification; wistful
(3) alliteration; humorous
(4) hyperbole; objective
  1. A well that “says” and “has learned” is given human acts: personification.
  2. The tap has replaced the old well and the rope that “once sang”: a quiet regret for the past = wistful/nostalgic.

Answer: (2) personification; wistful

TrapsTraps in literary RC

  • Taking a figurative line literally (the coin, the singing rope).
  • Choosing your own moral instead of the one the text draws.
  • Confusing the narrator’s feeling with another character’s (Meera vs her father).
  • Picking a strong tone (bitter, furious) for a gentle passage — literary passages in CUET are usually reflective.
  • In “what is NOT true of the protagonist”, an option that is true of a different character.
  • For a poem, ignoring the turn: then-versus-now is the commonest poem structure in the bank.

ConceptMethod

  1. Decide first whether the stem asks for SAME or OPPOSITE; write S or O. Antonym items outnumber synonym items.
  2. Give the word a charge: positive or negative. A synonym keeps the charge; an antonym flips it. This alone removes two options in most items.
  3. Use roots: bene-/eu- good, mal-/dys- bad, -phile lover, -phobe fear, loqu/voc speak, chron time, luc/lum light, ver truth, mell honey, caco- bad (sound).
  4. For an underlined word in a sentence, the sentence decides the sense: try each option in the slot.
  5. Matching lists: fix the one pair you are sure of, then strike options; two sure pairs usually settle it.
  6. Analogy: name the relation in a sentence (“X and Y mean the same”, “X is the opposite of Y”) and test each option with the same sentence.

TrapsTraps

  • The planted synonym in an antonym item (and vice versa) — the commonest trap of all.
  • Look-alike words: ingenious (clever) vs ingenuous (innocent); exalt vs exult; flagrant vs fragrant; imperious vs impervious.
  • A word with two senses: in a sentence item the sentence picks the sense (sophisticated machine vs person).
  • Same root, different charge: credulous (believes too easily) vs credible (believable).
  • When no option is a true antonym, choose the best available one — but know that NTA sometimes drops such an item: one insipid antonym item (prosaic / lacklustre / tasteless / weakness) had no correct option and NTA gave every candidate the marks.

ConceptRules that settle most spellings

  • i before e except after c (receive, believe) when the sound is “ee”; exceptions: seize, weird, either.
  • Double the final consonant of a short stressed syllable before -ed/-ing: refer → referred, but offer → offered.
  • Drop silent -e before a vowel suffix (hope → hoping) but keep it before a consonant (hopeful); keep it after soft c/g: noticeable, courageous.
  • Silent letters from Greek: pneumonia, psychology, rhythm.
  • Possessive pronouns take no apostrophe: yours, its, hers, ours (the bank asked “______ sincerely” → Yours).

Worked exampleCorrect spelling

Select the correctly spelt word.

(1) Accomodation
(2) Accommodation
(3) Acommodation
(4) Accommodasion
  1. Double c and double m: ac-com-mo-da-tion.

Answer: (2) Accommodation

Worked exampleHomophone pair

Fill in the pair: The guide warned us not to ______ the old fort’s walls, which the city plans to ______ next year.

(1) raze, raise
(2) raise, raze
(3) deface, raze
(4) raze, deface
  1. First blank: damage the walls — deface. Second: the city will demolish it — raze.
  2. “Raise” (lift) fits neither.

Answer: (3) deface, raze

Worked exampleDiscreet / discrete

Choose the word: Please be ______ about the surprise; she must not find out.

(1) discrete
(2) discreet
(3) descrete
(4) discreat
  1. Tactful, keeping a secret = discreet. Discrete = separate. Options 3 and 4 are misspellings.

Answer: (2) discreet

TrapsTraps

  • Two misspellings that look right together: read each letter group, not the word shape.
  • Homophones in a two-blank item are often offered in the swapped order: check each blank separately.
  • Successive (one after another) vs successful; caste vs cast; stationary (still) vs stationery (paper).
