ConceptConcentration terms and gas solubility

  • Molarity M = moles of solute / litres of solution (changes with temperature, because volume does). Molality m = moles of solute / kg of solvent (temperature-independent: the “more reliable” term). Mole fraction xA = nA/Σn, and the mole fractions add to 1. Mass %, volume %, ppm = parts per 106 by mass.
  • Molarity to molality: m = 1000M/(1000d − M·M2) with d in g/mL. Example: 20% (w/w) KI, d = 1.202: in 100 g solution 20 g KI = 0.1205 mol in 80 g water ⇒ m = 1.506 mol/kg.
  • Henry’s law: p = KHx (partial pressure of the gas over the solution is proportional to its mole fraction in the solution). A larger KH means a less soluble gas. KH rises with temperature, so gases are less soluble in warm water (fish prefer cold water).
  • Applications: soda bottles are sealed under high CO2 pressure; bends (N2 bubbles in the blood of divers surfacing fast; tanks use He-diluted air); anoxia at high altitude (low O2 partial pressure).
  • Dissolving a solid: endothermic dissolution ⇒ solubility rises with temperature (Le Chatelier); pressure has no real effect on solids. “Like dissolves like”: in n-octane, cyclohexane > CH3CN > CH3OH > NaCl.
  • Types: gas in gas (air), solid in gas (camphor in N2), liquid in solid (amalgam of Hg with Na), solid in solid (alloys, brass).

ConceptRaoult’s law, ideal and non-ideal solutions, azeotropes

  • For two volatile liquids pA = xAp°A, ptotal = p°B + (p°A − p°B)xA. The vapour is richer in the more volatile component: yA = pA/ptotal.
  • Ideal (obeys Raoult at all compositions): ΔmixH = 0, ΔmixV = 0, ΔmixS > 0, ΔmixG < 0. A–B forces equal A–A and B–B. Nearly ideal: n-hexane + n-heptane, bromoethane + chloroethane, benzene + toluene.
  • Positive deviation: A–B weaker, p higher than Raoult, ΔmixH > 0, ΔmixV > 0. Ethanol + water, ethanol + acetone, CS2 + acetone. Large ones give a minimum-boiling azeotrope (95% ethanol + water, 351 K).
  • Negative deviation: A–B stronger (often H-bonding), p lower, ΔmixH < 0, ΔmixV < 0. Chloroform + acetone, phenol + aniline, HNO3 + water (68% HNO3, a maximum-boiling azeotrope at 393.5 K), HCl + water.
  • An azeotrope boils at constant composition, so it cannot be separated by fractional distillation.

DerivationThe van’t Hoff factor from the degree of dissociation or association

i = (observed colligative property)/(calculated) = (normal molar mass)/(observed molar mass) = (particles after)/(particles before).

  1. Dissociation into n ions, degree α: 1 → (1 − α) + nα particles, so i = 1 + (n − 1)α, α = (i − 1)/(n − 1). For AxBy, n = x + y.
  2. Association nA ⇌ An, degree α: 1 → (1 − α) + α/n, so i = 1 − (1 − 1/n)α. Dimerisation (n = 2): i = 1 − α/2, the lowest possible value 0.5.
  3. Complete dissociation: NaCl, KCl 2; K2SO4, MgCl2, BaCl2 3; AlCl3, K3[Fe(CN)6], Al(NO3)3 4; K4[Fe(CN)6], Al2(SO4)3 5. Glucose, urea, sucrose 1. Acetic acid in water: between 1 and 2; in benzene (dimer) about 0.5.
  4. So observed molar mass is lower than true for electrolytes (dissociation) and higher for associating solutes.

DiagramWhy a solute raises the boiling point and lowers the freezing point; osmosis

A non-volatile solute lowers the vapour pressure at every temperature; osmosis is the same lowered escaping tendency of the solvent, seen through a membrane. Isotonic solutions have equal π: blood is isotonic with 0.9% (w/v) NaCl. In a stronger NaCl solution (hypertonic) red cells shrink; in a weaker one they swell and burst.

TrapsTraps

  • Osmotic pressure uses molarity; ΔTb and ΔTf use molality.
  • Larger KH = less soluble gas. Solubility of a gas falls on heating.
  • Positive deviation ⇒ minimum-boiling azeotrope (maximum vapour pressure). The words cross over.
  • For association i < 1 and the observed molar mass is too high; for dissociation the reverse.
  • Reverse osmosis needs a pressure larger than π on the solution side.
  • In a mixing problem, find the total moles and the new total volume before using π = CRT.

ConceptGalvanic cells and electrode potentials

  • A galvanic cell turns the Gibbs energy of a spontaneous redox reaction into electrical work. Anode: oxidation, negative terminal (left in the cell notation). Cathode: reduction, positive. Notation Zn(s) | Zn2+(aq) || Cu2+(aq) | Cu(s); || is the salt bridge.
  • The salt bridge (KCl or KNO3 in agar) completes the circuit and keeps both solutions neutral. Remove it and the voltage drops to zero.
  • If an external voltage greater than Ecell (1.1 V for Daniell) is applied in opposition, the current reverses and the cell becomes electrolytic (Cu dissolves, Zn deposits).
  • Standard electrode potentials are reduction potentials measured against the SHE (0 V). E°cell = E°cathode − E°anode = E°right − E°left; positive means spontaneous.
  • More negative E° = stronger reducing agent (Li, −3.05 V, the strongest in water; K −2.93, Mg −2.37, Zn −0.76). More positive = stronger oxidising agent (F2 +2.87 V the strongest; Li+ the weakest). A metal displaces those below it: CuSO4 cannot be kept in a zinc vessel.
  • E° is intensive: multiplying a half-equation by 2 does not change it. Combining two half-reactions into a third one uses ΔG°, not E°: n3E°3 = n1E°1 + n2E°2.

DerivationFrom the Nernst equation to K and ΔG°

Used for every “equilibrium constant from E°” and “effect of concentration” question.

  1. Ecell = E°cell − (2.303RT/nF) log Q, and 2.303 × 8.314 × 298/96 500 = 0.0591 V.
  2. At equilibrium the cell is “dead”: Ecell = 0 and Q = K, so E°cell = (0.059/n) log K.
  3. Electrical work = charge × voltage: ΔG = −nFEcell; with ΔG° = −2.303RT log K the same relation follows.
  4. Consequence: raising the product-ion concentration (Zn2+ or Mg2+) lowers Ecell; raising the reactant ion (Cu2+, Ag+) raises it.
  5. Hydrogen electrode in a solution of pH x: E = −0.059 pH (at 1 bar H2).

ConceptConductance of solutions

  • κ (conductivity) decreases on dilution: fewer ions per cm3. Λm increases on dilution: it is the conductance of the whole mole, and ions are freer (strong) or more dissociated (weak).
  • Strong electrolyte: Λm rises slowly, linearly with √c; extrapolating to c = 0 gives Λ°m. Weak electrolyte: Λm rises steeply at low c, so Λ°m comes from Kohlrausch.
  • Electronic (metallic) conductance falls as temperature rises; electrolytic conductance rises with temperature. Order of conductivity: Cu, Ag, Au (107 S m−1) >> Ge, Si (semiconductors) >> glass (10−16).
  • Conductivity is measured with an AC Wheatstone bridge (DC would electrolyse the solution).
  • H+ (349.6) and OH− (199.1 S cm2 mol−1) have by far the largest λ°, so acids and bases conduct best: among 0.01 M salts, HCl > NaOH > KCl > NaCl > CH3COONa.

TrapsTraps

  • E° does not double when a half-equation is doubled, but ΔG° and n do.
  • In Q, write products over reactants and leave out solids; the 0.059 is divided by n, the number of electrons in the balanced equation.
  • Λm rises on dilution but κ falls: statement questions swap them.
  • Units: κ in S cm−1, Λm in S cm2 mol−1, cell constant in cm−1.
  • In a galvanic cell the anode is negative; in an electrolytic cell the anode is positive. Oxidation is at the anode in both.
  • Aqueous NaCl gives Cl2, not O2, at the anode (overpotential), even though water’s E° suggests O2.

ConceptRate, rate law, order and molecularity

  • For aA + bB → cC + dD: rate = −(1/a)d[A]/dt = −(1/b)d[B]/dt = (1/c)d[C]/dt. Example: in 5Br− + BrO3− + 6H+ → 3Br2 + 3H2O, rate = one-fifth of the rate of disappearance of Br−. Average rate over an interval; instantaneous rate = slope of the tangent.
  • Rate law rate = k[A]x[B]y; x, y are found by experiment, not from the coefficients. Order = x + y: may be 0, a fraction or negative. CHCl3 + Cl2: rate = k[CHCl3][Cl2]1/2, order 3/2 (a complex reaction, so molecularity has no meaning for the overall equation).
  • Molecularity: number of species colliding in an elementary step; always a whole number 1, 2 or (rarely) 3, never zero. For a complex reaction the order is decided by the slowest step.
  • Pseudo-first order: one reactant in large excess, e.g. hydrolysis of ethyl acetate in water, inversion of cane sugar (rate = k[C12H22O11]).
  • k depends on temperature and catalyst, not on concentration. Zero-order examples: decomposition of NH3 on hot Pt, of HI on gold. First-order: all radioactive decay, N2O5 → N2O4, H2O2 decomposition, hydrogenation of ethene (rate = k[C2H4]).
  • Changing the volume of a gas changes every concentration: halving the volume doubles each [ ] and multiplies the rate by 2order.

DerivationIntegrated first-order equation and half-life

The source of every “percent completed” question.

  1. −d[R]/dt = k[R] ⇒ d[R]/[R] = −k dt. Integrate from [R]0 at t = 0: ln[R] = ln[R]0 − kt.
  2. So k = (2.303/t) log([R]0/[R]), and [R] = [R]0e−kt.
  3. Half-life: put [R] = [R]0/2: t½ = 2.303 log 2/k = 0.693/k.
  4. 90% complete: [R]0/[R] = 10, t = 2.303/k; 99.9%: ratio 1000, t = 3 × 2.303/k. Hence t99.9% = 3t90%.
  5. Zero order: d[R] = −k dt ⇒ [R] = [R]0 − kt, t½ = [R]0/2k.

ConceptTemperature, activation energy, catalysts, collision theory

  • Arrhenius: k = Ae−Ea/RT; ln k = ln A − Ea/RT. Plot of ln k vs 1/T: slope −Ea/R (log k vs 1/T: slope −Ea/2.303R), intercept ln A.
  • Two temperatures: log(k2/k1) = (Ea/2.303R)(T2 − T1)/(T1T2).
  • e−Ea/RT is the fraction of molecules with energy ≥ Ea. A 10 K rise roughly doubles k (temperature coefficient kT+10/kT ≈ 2–3). The larger Ea, the more sensitive k is to temperature. If Ea = 0, k = A; if Ea = 2.303RT, k/A = e−2.303 = 0.1.
  • Catalyst: provides a path of lower Ea; unchanged in mass and composition at the end; does not change ΔH, ΔG or K; speeds forward and back reactions equally, so equilibrium is reached sooner. MnO2 lets KClO3 decompose at a lower temperature.
  • Collision theory (bimolecular): rate = PZABe−Ea/RT. ZAB = collision frequency; P = probability or steric factor (only correctly oriented collisions react). Limitation: treats molecules as hard spheres and ignores their structure. Raising concentration raises the collision frequency, not the fraction of effective collisions.

TrapsTraps

  • Order comes from the rate law, not the balanced equation; molecularity cannot be zero or fractional.
  • If k has units of rate (mol L−1 s−1) the reaction is zero order; s−1 means first order.
  • First-order t½ does not depend on [R]0; zero-order t½ is proportional to it.
  • Slope of log k vs 1/T is −Ea/2.303R, of ln k vs 1/T is −Ea/R: check which log the question uses.
  • Temperatures in kelvin; Ea comes out in J mol−1 when R = 8.314.
  • The rate of a first-order reaction falls as the reaction proceeds; k does not.

ConceptConfigurations and general trends of the 3d series

  • Transition element: partly filled d subshell in the atom or a common ion. (n−1)d1–10ns1–2. Exceptions Cr 3d54s1, Cu 3d104s1 (half-filled and full d). Zn, Cd, Hg (d10 in atom and ions) are d-block but not transition elements; Sc is one (Sc3+ is d0, but Sc is 3d1).
  • Ions lose 4s before 3d: Fe2+ 3d6, Fe3+ 3d5, Cu2+ 3d9, Co3+ 3d6, Mn2+ 3d5, Cr3+ 3d3, V3+ 3d2, Ti3+ 3d1.
  • Radii fall across the series then level off (extra d electrons shield the extra nuclear charge). 4d and 5d of the same group are nearly equal (Zr 160, Hf 159 pm) because of the lanthanoid contraction.
  • Melting points high (strong metallic bonding by d electrons), peaking near d5; Mn and Tc anomalously low; W highest; Hg liquid. Zn has the lowest enthalpy of atomisation (no unpaired d).
  • Ionisation enthalpy rises slowly. The 2nd IE is high for Cr and Cu (removing from stable d5, d10); the 3rd IE is high for Mn (d5) and Zn.
  • Oxidation states: variable because ns and (n−1)d energies are close; most at Mn (+2 to +7); the maximum equals the number of 4s + 3d electrons up to Mn. Sc only +3; Zn only +2; Cu the only one with a common +1. Higher states are stabilised by O and F (MnO4−, CrO3, VF5).
  • Oxides: lower states basic, highest acidic. MnO basic, Mn2O7 acidic; CrO basic, Cr2O3 amphoteric, CrO3 acidic; V2O5 amphoteric.

ConceptElectrode potentials, magnetism, colour, catalysis, alloys

  • E°(M2+/M) is negative for all 3d metals except Cu (+0.34 V): the high atomisation plus ionisation energy of Cu is not repaid by its hydration enthalpy, so Cu does not liberate H2 from acids. Mn, Ni, Zn are more negative than the trend (stable d5, highest hydration of Ni2+, d10).
  • E°(M3+/M2+): Mn3+ (+1.57) and Co3+ (+1.97) are strong oxidants (Mn3+, d4, gains an electron to reach the stable d5 Mn2+); Cr2+ (−0.41) is a strong reductant (Cr3+ is stable t2g3); Fe3+ (d5) is the stable state of iron. Cu+ disproportionates in water (Cu2+ has much larger hydration enthalpy).
  • Spin-only magnetic moment μ = √(n(n+2)) BM, n = unpaired electrons: 1 → 1.73, 2 → 2.83, 3 → 3.87, 4 → 4.90, 5 → 5.92. Maximum for d5 (Mn2+, Fe3+).
  • Colour: d–d transitions absorb visible light; the colour seen is the complement. d0 (Sc3+, Ti4+) and d10 (Cu+, Zn2+) are colourless. KMnO4 and K2Cr2O7 are d0 yet intensely coloured: charge transfer, not d–d.
  • Catalysts (variable oxidation states, surface): V2O5 (contact process), finely divided Fe (Haber), Ni (hydrogenation), TiCl4 + Al(CH3)3 (Ziegler–Natta, polythene), PdCl2 (Wacker: ethene → ethanal), Fe3+ (I− + S2O82−). Mn2+ autocatalyses the KMnO4–oxalic acid reaction.
  • Interstitial compounds (H, C, N trapped in the lattice, e.g. TiC, Mn4N, Fe3H): non-stoichiometric, very hard, high m.p., metallic conductivity, chemically inert. Alloys form readily because the radii are within about 15% of each other (brass Cu–Zn, bronze Cu–Sn, steels).

ConceptLanthanoids and actinoids

  • Lanthanoids Ce–Lu fill 4f; La is [Xe]5d16s2, Ce [Xe]4f15d16s2, Gd 4f75d16s2. Common state +3 for all. +4: Ce4+ (oxidant, f0), Tb4+ (f7); +2: Eu2+ (f7), Yb2+ (f14, diamagnetic) are reductants. Promethium is the only synthetic one.
  • Lanthanoid contraction: steady fall in atomic and Ln3+ radius from La to Lu, caused by the poor shielding of 4f electrons. Results: basicity of Ln(OH)3 falls from La(OH)3 to Lu(OH)3; Zr/Hf (and Nb/Ta, Mo/W) have almost identical radii and occur together; separating lanthanoids is hard.
  • Lanthanoids are silvery, soft-ish metals that tarnish in air, liberate H2 from dilute acids, burn to Ln2O3. Most Ln3+ ions are coloured (f–f); La3+ and Lu3+ are not.
  • Mischmetall: ~95% lanthanoid metals + ~5% Fe with traces of S, C, Ca, Al; Mg-based alloy with it is used in bullets, shells and lighter flints.
  • Actinoids (Th–Lr): all radioactive; 5f, 6d and 7s are close in energy, so they show more oxidation states (up to +7 for Np, Pu) than lanthanoids; actinoid contraction is greater (5f shields even more poorly than 4f). Th, Pa, U occur naturally; the later ones are synthetic.

ConceptWerner’s theory and the vocabulary

  • Primary valency = oxidation state (ionisable, satisfied by anions outside the bracket); secondary valency = coordination number (non-ionisable, fixed geometry). CoCl3·6NH3 gives 3 AgCl ⇒ [Co(NH3)6]Cl3; ·5NH3 gives 2 ⇒ [Co(NH3)5Cl]Cl2; ·4NH3 gives 1 ⇒ [Co(NH3)4Cl2]Cl.
  • Double salt (Mohr’s salt, potash alum) dissociates completely in water; a complex keeps its entity. Ions in solution: [Cr(NH3)6]Cl3 → 4.
  • Central atom is a Lewis acid, ligands Lewis bases. Denticity: unidentate (Cl−, NH3, H2O), didentate (en, C2O42−), hexadentate (EDTA4−). A polydentate ligand that forms a ring is a chelate; chelates are more stable. Ambidentate: two possible donor atoms but binds through one (NO2−/ONO−, SCN−/NCS−, CN−).
  • Coordination number = number of donor atoms bound (not ligands): [Fe(C2O4)3]3− 6, [Fe(CO)5] 5, [Co(en)3]3+ 6.
  • Homoleptic: one kind of ligand ([Co(NH3)6]3+); heteroleptic: more than one ([Co(NH3)4Cl2]+).

  • Ligands are point charges; in an octahedral field the d orbitals split into t2g (lower, −0.4Δo) and eg (upper, +0.6Δo). Tetrahedral: e below t2, Δt = (4/9)Δo, so tetrahedral complexes are high spin.
  • Spectrochemical series (increasing Δ): I− < Br− < SCN− < Cl− < S2− < F− < OH− < C2O42− < H2O < NCS− < edta4− < NH3 < en < CN− < CO.
  • d4–d7 octahedral: Δo < P (pairing energy) gives high spin t2g3eg1…; Δo > P gives low spin t2g4… CFSE = (−0.4x + 0.6y)Δo for t2gxegy (+ P per extra pair): low-spin d6 is −2.4Δo.
  • Colour: an electron jumps t2g → eg by absorbing light of energy Δo; we see the complementary colour. Stronger ligand ⇒ larger Δ ⇒ shorter wavelength absorbed. [Ti(H2O)6]3+ absorbs green-yellow (~498 nm) and looks violet; anhydrous CuSO4 is white (no ligand field from water), CuSO4·5H2O blue; NH3 on Cu2+ gives deep blue [Cu(NH3)4]2+.
  • Limitations of CFT: ligands treated as point charges, so it cannot explain why neutral NH3 and CO are stronger than anionic F−; no covalent bonding.
  • Metal carbonyls: [Ni(CO)4] tetrahedral, [Fe(CO)5] trigonal bipyramidal, [Cr(CO)6] octahedral, [Mn2(CO)10], [Co2(CO)8]. Synergic bonding: CO donates its lone pair to the metal (σ) and the metal donates d electrons back into CO’s π* (back-bonding), so M–C strengthens and C–O weakens; more back-donation (more electron-rich metal: [V(CO)6]− > [Cr(CO)6] > [Mn(CO)6]+) means lower C–O bond order.

ConceptThe carbonyl group and nucleophilic addition

  • C=O: carbon sp2, trigonal planar (120°), one σ and one π bond, polar (C δ+, O δ−). Nucleophiles attack the carbon; the carbon becomes sp3 (tetrahedral) in the product.
  • Reactivity: aldehydes > ketones (fewer +I alkyl groups, less crowding). HCHO > CH3CHO > CH3CH2CHO > CH3COCH3 > CH3COC2H5; aromatic aldehydes and ketones are less reactive than aliphatic ones (ring resonance reduces the δ+). Electron-withdrawing groups on the ring raise it: p-nitrobenzaldehyde > benzaldehyde > p-tolualdehyde.
  • Additions: HCN (base-catalysed) → cyanohydrin (CH3CHO → lactic acid after hydrolysis); NaHSO3 → crystalline bisulphite adduct (separates aldehydes); alcohols, dry HCl → hemiacetal → acetal (gem-dialkoxy; ketones with ethylene glycol give ketals); Grignard reagents → alcohols.
  • Ammonia derivatives (addition then loss of water, pH about 3.5): NH2OH → oxime; NH2NH2 → hydrazone; C6H5NHNH2 → phenylhydrazone; 2,4-DNP (2,4-dinitrophenylhydrazine) → orange-yellow 2,4-DNP derivative; NH2NHCONH2 (semicarbazide) → semicarbazone.
  • Boiling points: alcohols and acids (H-bonded) > aldehydes and ketones (dipole–dipole) > ethers ≈ alkanes of similar mass. Carboxylic acids are H-bonded dimers even in the vapour.

ConceptAcidity of carboxylic acids

  • RCOOH is more acidic than phenol because the carboxylate’s two resonance forms put the charge on two equal oxygens.
  • Electron-withdrawing groups raise acidity, more strongly the more electronegative, the more numerous and the closer they are: CF3COOH (0.23) > CCl3COOH (0.64) > Cl2CHCOOH (1.26) > FCH2COOH (2.6) > ClCH2COOH (2.87) > HCOOH (3.75) > C6H5COOH (4.19) > CH3COOH (4.76) > CH3CH2COOH (4.88). (pKa in brackets.)
  • 2-chlorobutanoic > 3-chlorobutanoic > 4-chlorobutanoic acid. Electron-donating groups lower acidity. On benzoic acid, o/p-NO2 raise acidity; p-OCH3, p-CH3 lower it. Phenylacetic acid is weaker than benzoic acid.
  • Uses: HCHO (40% solution is formalin: preserving specimens, disinfectant), acetone solvent, sodium benzoate food preservative, esters for flavours.

ConceptStructure, classification, preparation

  • N is sp3 with a lone pair: pyramidal, C–N–C ≈ 108° in trimethylamine. 1° RNH2, 2° R2NH, 3° R3N; quaternary salt R4N+X−. Names: CH2=CHCH2NH2 prop-2-en-1-amine (allylamine); (CH3)2NH N-methylmethanamine; C6H5N(CH3)2 N,N-dimethylaniline.
  • Reduction of nitro compounds: Fe/HCl (preferred: FeCl2 formed is hydrolysed, releasing HCl, so little acid is needed), Sn/HCl, H2/Pd.
  • Ammonolysis of alkyl halides: gives a mixture of 1°, 2°, 3° and quaternary salt (excess NH3 favours 1°).
  • Reduction of nitriles (LiAlH4, or Na/ethanol: Mendius) and amides (LiAlH4) gives amines with the same carbon count (nitrile route adds one C to an alkyl halide).
  • Gabriel phthalimide synthesis: phthalimide + KOH → potassium phthalimide + RX → N-alkylphthalimide → (NaOH) RNH2. Pure primary aliphatic amines only: aryl halides do not substitute, so aniline cannot be made this way.

ConceptBasic strength

  • Aqueous order is set by three effects together: +I of alkyl groups (more is better), solvation of the ammonium ion by H-bonding (more N–H is better), and steric crowding. Ethyl series: (C2H5)2NH > (C2H5)3N > C2H5NH2 > NH3; methyl series: (CH3)2NH > CH3NH2 > (CH3)3N > NH3.
  • Gas phase (no solvation): 3° > 2° > 1° > NH3.
  • Aryl amines are much weaker: aniline’s lone pair is delocalised into the ring. Benzylamine (CH2 between ring and N) behaves like an alkylamine.
  • Substituted anilines: electron donors raise basicity (p-toluidine, p-anisidine > aniline), withdrawers lower it (p-nitroaniline < aniline). Diphenylamine is weaker still. Amides (CH3CONH2) are hardly basic.

ConceptAmino acids and proteins

  • All natural ones (except glycine, which is achiral) are L-α-amino acids. In water they exist as zwitterions H3N+–CHR–COO−: amphoteric, high melting, water-soluble.
  • Essential (from diet): valine, leucine, isoleucine, arginine, lysine, threonine, methionine, phenylalanine, tryptophan, histidine. Non-essential: glycine, alanine, serine, glutamic acid… Acidic: aspartic and glutamic acid; basic: lysine, arginine, histidine.
  • Peptide bond −CO–NH− links the −COOH of one to the −NH2 of the next with loss of water.
  • Fibrous proteins (keratin of hair and wool, myosin of muscle) are insoluble, with parallel chains held by H-bonds and disulphide bonds; globular (insulin, albumin) are soluble.
  • Structure: primary = amino-acid sequence; secondary = α-helix or β-pleated sheet, held by hydrogen bonds between C=O and N–H; tertiary = overall folding (H-bonds, disulphide, van der Waals, electrostatic); quaternary = arrangement of sub-units.
  • Denaturation (heat, pH): secondary and tertiary structure lost, primary unchanged, biological activity lost: boiling an egg, curdling milk.
  • Enzymes are (mostly globular) proteins: very specific catalysts that lower activation energy. NCERT’s example: Ea for the hydrolysis of sucrose is 6.22 kJ mol−1 with acid and 2.15 kJ mol−1 with the enzyme sucrase. Those are the figures NCERT prints and a CUET question would quote, but they are implausibly small (the measured values are of the order of 100 kJ mol−1 for the acid route and a few tens with the enzyme): learn the direction, enzyme much lower. lock-and-key action at the active site. Invertase: sucrose → glucose + fructose; maltase: maltose → glucose; zymase: glucose → ethanol; urease: urea → NH3 + CO2.

ConceptNucleic acids

  • Polymers of nucleotides. Nucleoside = base + sugar (base at C-1′); nucleotide = nucleoside + phosphate (at C-5′). Nucleotides join by phosphodiester links between C-5′ and C-3′.
  • DNA: sugar β-D-2-deoxyribose (no OH at C-2′); bases A, G (purines), C, T (pyrimidines); double helix (Watson–Crick), A=T two H-bonds, G≡C three. RNA: β-D-ribose; A, G, C, U; single strand; three types: messenger, ribosomal, transfer RNA.
  • DNA carries heredity (replication); RNA directs protein synthesis. Chromosomes are made of nucleic acids and proteins. DNA fingerprinting: forensics, paternity, identifying bodies, racial groups.

Worked exampleInvert sugar

Hydrolysis of sucrose gives a solution whose optical rotation is

  1. (1) dextrorotatory, because glucose is dextrorotatory
  2. (2) laevorotatory, because fructose (−92.4°) outweighs glucose (+52.5°)
  3. (3) zero, because the products are a racemic mixture
  4. (4) unchanged from that of sucrose
  1. Sucrose (+66.5°) → equimolar glucose (+52.5°) + fructose (−92.4°); net about −20°.
  2. The sign inverts: hence “invert sugar” and the enzyme “invertase”.

Answer: (2) laevorotatory, because fructose (−92.4°) outweighs glucose (+52.5°)

Worked exampleGlucose reactions

Which reaction shows that the six carbons of glucose form a straight chain?

  1. (1) bromine water → gluconic acid
  2. (2) HI, prolonged heating → n-hexane
  3. (3) acetic anhydride → pentaacetate
  4. (4) HNO3 → saccharic acid

A DNA segment has 10 A–T pairs and 6 G–C pairs. The number of hydrogen bonds between its strands is

  1. (1) 32
  2. (2) 48
  3. (3) 38
  4. (4) 36

Worked exampleProtein structure

Which statement is correct?

  1. (1) Denaturation destroys the primary structure of a protein
  2. (2) The α-helix is held by hydrogen bonds between the C=O and N–H groups of the chain
  3. (3) Keratin is a globular protein
  4. (4) Glycine is optically active

TrapsTraps

  • Sucrose is non-reducing (both anomeric carbons are tied up); maltose and lactose are reducing.
  • D and L describe configuration, (+) and (−) the measured rotation: D-fructose is laevorotatory.
  • Amylose is the water-soluble part of starch (15–20%), amylopectin the insoluble part.
  • Denaturation leaves the primary structure intact.
  • Uracil is in RNA only, thymine in DNA only; DNA’s sugar differs from RNA’s only at C-2′.
  • Glycine is the one achiral amino acid; vitamin B12 contains cobalt.
