Question 1Solutions
A solution of 3.05 g of benzoic acid (C6H5COOH, M = 122 g mol−1) in 50 g of benzene lowers its freezing point by 1.40 K (Kf of benzene = 5.12 K kg mol−1). If benzoic acid exists partly as dimers in benzene, the percentage of molecules that associate is
  1. 54.7%
  2. 9.4%
  3. 90.6%
  4. 45.3%
Concept to recallObserved molar mass Mobs = Kf wB × 1000 / (ΔTf wA); i = Mnormal/Mobs. For dimerisation 2A → A2, i = 1 − α/2, so α = 2(1 − i).
  1. Mobs = 5.12 × 3.05 × 1000 / (1.40 × 50) = 15616/70 = 223 g mol−1.
  2. i = 122/223 = 0.547 (less than 1: association).
  3. For dimers, 1 mol of A gives (1 − α) + α/2 particles: i = 1 − α/2.
  4. α = 2(1 − 0.547) = 0.906, i.e. 90.6% of the molecules dimerise.

Answer: option (3) — 90.6%

Trap45.3% stops at 1 − i and forgets the factor 2 that dimerisation brings; 54.7% reads i itself as α; 9.4% is the fraction left unassociated.
Question 2Solutions
A 0.010 m aqueous solution of Na3PO4 freezes at −0.062 °C. Taking Kf of water = 1.86 K kg mol−1, the degree of dissociation of the salt is
  1. 77.8%
  2. 83.3%
  3. 58.3%
  4. 33.3%
Concept to recallΔTf = i Kf m. A salt giving n ions: i = 1 + (n − 1)α, so α = (i − 1)/(n − 1). Na3PO4 → 3Na+ + PO43−: n = 4.
  1. i = ΔTf/(Kf m) = 0.062/(1.86 × 0.010) = 3.33.
  2. n = 4 ions per formula unit, so 3.33 = 1 + 3α.
  3. α = 2.33/3 = 0.778 = 77.8%.

Answer: option (1) — 77.8%

Trap83.3% divides i by n; 58.3% divides (i − 1) by n instead of n − 1; 33.3% reads the excess of i over 3 as the dissociation.

Benzene (p° = 75 torr) and toluene (p° = 22 torr) form an ideal solution. For a solution made from 78 g of benzene and 46 g of toluene, the mole fraction of benzene in the vapour in equilibrium with it is
  1. 0.667
  2. 0.629
  3. 0.128
  4. 0.872
Concept to recallRaoult: pi = xip°i; Dalton: yi = pi/ptotal. The vapour is richer in the more volatile component.
  1. n(benzene) = 78/78 = 1 mol; n(toluene) = 46/92 = 0.5 mol; xB = 1/1.5 = 0.667.
  2. pB = 0.667 × 75 = 50.0 torr; pT = 0.333 × 22 = 7.33 torr; ptotal = 57.3 torr.
  3. yB = 50.0/57.3 = 0.872.

Answer: option (4) — 0.872

Trap0.667 is the mole fraction in the liquid; 0.629 is the mass fraction 78/124; 0.128 is the toluene vapour fraction.
Question 4Solutions
Consider the following statements.
  1. (A) Osmotic pressure is the preferred colligative property for finding the molar mass of a protein, since it is measured at room temperature and is sizeable even for very dilute solutions.
  2. (B) In reverse osmosis a pressure larger than the osmotic pressure is applied to the solution side, so that solvent flows out of the solution through the membrane.
  3. (C) 0.1 M glucose and 0.1 M NaCl solutions are isotonic.
  4. (D) A red blood cell placed in 0.5% (w/v) NaCl solution swells.
  5. (E) The relative lowering of vapour pressure equals the mole fraction of the solvent.
Choose the correct answer from the options given below:
  1. (A) and (B) only
  2. (A), (B) and (D) only
  3. (A), (C) and (E) only
  4. (B), (C) and (D) only
Concept to recallπ = iCRT is large even at low C, so it suits macromolecules that are heat-sensitive. Isotonic means equal iC. Blood cells are isotonic with 0.9% NaCl (normal saline): a weaker solution is hypotonic, water enters and the cell swells. Raoult: (p° − p)/p° = xsolute.
  1. (A) true.
  2. (B) true: that is how sea water is desalinated.
  3. (C) false: NaCl gives two ions (i ≈ 2), so its π is about twice that of glucose.
  4. (D) true: 0.5% is hypotonic to the 0.9% inside the cell; water flows in.
  5. (E) false: it equals the mole fraction of the solute.

Answer: option (2) — (A), (B) and (D) only

Trap“(A) and (B) only” misses (D) by reading 0.5% as stronger than the cell fluid; (C) forgets the van’t Hoff factor; (E) names the wrong component.

  1. 0.41%
  2. 0.81%
  3. 1.63%
  4. 3.25%
Concept to recallIsotonic solutions have equal osmotic pressure π = iCRT, i.e. equal i×C. NaCl gives two particles (i = 2); glucose does not dissociate (i = 1).

At 298 K a conductivity cell filled with 0.1 M KCl solution (conductivity κ = 1.29 S m−1) shows a resistance of 100 Ω. The same cell, washed and filled with 0.025 M acetic acid, shows a resistance of 5160 Ω.

Limiting molar conductivities at 298 K: λ°(H+) = 349.6 S cm2 mol−1, λ°(CH3COO−) = 40.9 S cm2 mol−1. For a weak electrolyte, the degree of dissociation at concentration c is taken as α = Λmc/Λ°m.

The cell constant of the conductivity cell is
  1. 129 cm−1
  2. 0.775 cm−1
  3. 0.0129 cm−1
  4. 1.29 cm−1
Concept to recallκ = G*/R, so the cell constant G* = l/A = κR (found once with a KCl solution of known κ).
  1. G* = κR = 1.29 S m−1 × 100 Ω = 129 m−1.
  2. 1 m−1 = 0.01 cm−1, so G* = 1.29 cm−1.

Answer: option (4) — 1.29 cm−1

Trap129 cm−1 keeps the number in m−1 but writes cm−1; 0.775 is 1/G* (R/κ upside-down); 0.0129 is κ/R.

At 298 K a conductivity cell filled with 0.1 M KCl solution (conductivity κ = 1.29 S m−1) shows a resistance of 100 Ω. The same cell, washed and filled with 0.025 M acetic acid, shows a resistance of 5160 Ω.

The molar conductivity of the 0.025 M acetic acid solution is

  3. 1000 S cm2 mol−1
  4. 390.5 S cm2 mol−1

Question 8Electrochemistry
Passage: conductance of acetic acid (Q6–8)

At 298 K a conductivity cell filled with 0.1 M KCl solution (conductivity κ = 1.29 S m−1) shows a resistance of 100 Ω. The same cell, washed and filled with 0.025 M acetic acid, shows a resistance of 5160 Ω.

Limiting molar conductivities at 298 K: λ°(H+) = 349.6 S cm2 mol−1, λ°(CH3COO−) = 40.9 S cm2 mol−1. For a weak electrolyte, the degree of dissociation at concentration c is taken as α = Λmc/Λ°m.

The dissociation constant Ka of acetic acid, from these data, is closest to
  1. 6.4 × 10−4
  2. 2.6 × 10−2
  3. 1.7 × 10−5
  4. 6.7 × 10−4
Concept to recallKohlrausch: Λ°m(CH3COOH) = λ°(H+) + λ°(CH3COO−). Then α = Λm/Λ°m and Ostwald: Ka = cα2/(1 − α).
  1. Λ°m = 349.6 + 40.9 = 390.5 S cm2 mol−1.
  2. α = 10/390.5 = 0.0256.
  3. Ka = 0.025 × (0.0256)2/(1 − 0.0256) = 1.7 × 10−5.

Answer: option (3) — 1.7 × 10−5

Trap6.7 × 10−4 leaves c out of Ostwald’s law; 6.4 × 10−4 is cα, the [H+]; 2.6 × 10−2 is α itself.
Question 9Electrochemistry
For the cell Ni(s) | Ni2+(0.010 M) || Ag+(0.0020 M) | Ag(s) at 298 K, E°(Ni2+/Ni) = −0.25 V and E°(Ag+/Ag) = +0.80 V. Taking 2.303RT/F = 0.059 V and F = 96500 C mol−1, ΔrG for the cell reaction (as written for 2 mol of electrons) is
  1. −222 kJ mol−1
  2. −203 kJ mol−1
  3. −91.7 kJ mol−1
  4. −183 kJ mol−1
Concept to recallEcell = E°cell − (0.059/n) log Q, with Q written from the balanced reaction (concentrations raised to their coefficients). Then ΔrG = −nFEcell.
  1. Ni + 2Ag+ → Ni2+ + 2Ag; n = 2; E°cell = 0.80 − (−0.25) = 1.05 V.
  2. Q = [Ni2+]/[Ag+]2 = 0.010/(0.0020)2 = 2500; log Q = 3.40.
  3. Ecell = 1.05 − (0.059/2)(3.40) = 1.05 − 0.100 = 0.950 V.
  4. ΔrG = −2 × 96500 × 0.950 = −1.83 × 105 J = −183 kJ mol−1.

Answer: option (4) — −183 kJ mol−1

Trap−222 kJ inverts Q (adds the log term); −203 kJ is ΔrG°, using E° and ignoring the concentrations; −91.7 kJ takes n = 1 in −nFE.
Question 10Electrochemistry
Consider the following statements.
  1. (A) For a strong electrolyte, Λm = Λ°m − A√c, so a plot of Λm against √c is a straight line.
  2. (B) Λ°m of acetic acid is obtained by extrapolating its Λm–√c plot to zero concentration.
  3. (C) The quantity of charge that deposits 108 g of silver from AgNO3 deposits 31.75 g of copper from CuSO4.
  4. (D) The molar conductivity of a strong electrolyte decreases on dilution.
  5. (E) The conductivity κ of a solution decreases on dilution because the number of ions per unit volume falls.
Choose the correct answer from the options given below:
  1. (A), (C) and (D) only
  2. (A), (C) and (E) only
  3. (A), (B) and (D) only
  4. (B), (C) and (E) only
Concept to recallDebye–Hückel–Onsager: Λm = Λ°m − A√c for strong electrolytes only. For a weak electrolyte Λm rises steeply at low c, so Λ°m comes from Kohlrausch’s law. Faraday: 1 F deposits 1 mol of Ag but ½ mol (31.75 g) of Cu.
  1. (A) true.
  2. (B) false: the curve for a weak electrolyte cannot be extrapolated; use Kohlrausch’s law.
  3. (C) true: 108 g Ag is 1 F; 1 F gives 63.5/2 = 31.75 g Cu.
  4. (D) false: Λm rises on dilution for every electrolyte.
  5. (E) true.

Question 11Chemical Kinetics
The rate constant of a reaction is 1.5 × 10−3 s−1 at 300 K and its activation energy is 52 kJ mol−1. Its rate constant at 330 K is closest to (R = 8.314 J K−1 mol−1)
  1. 2.3 × 10−4 s−1
  2. 1.5 × 10−3 s−1
  3. 1.0 × 10−2 s−1
  4. 1.2 × 10−1 s−1
Concept to recalllog(k2/k1) = (Ea/2.303R)(T2 − T1)/(T1T2), with Ea in J mol−1.
  1. Ea/2.303R = 52000/(2.303 × 8.314) = 2716 K.
  2. (T2 − T1)/(T1T2) = 30/(300 × 330) = 3.03 × 10−4 K−1.
  3. log(k2/k1) = 0.823 ⇒ k2/k1 = 6.65.
  4. k2 = 6.65 × 1.5 × 10−3 = 1.0 × 10−2 s−1.

Answer: option (3) — 1.0 × 10−2 s−1

Trap1.2 × 10−1 uses log without the 2.303 (ratio 79); 2.3 × 10−4 divides by the ratio, as though k fell on heating; 1.5 × 10−3 (no change) puts Ea in kJ against R in J.
Question 12Chemical Kinetics
A first-order reaction is 20% complete in 10 minutes. How long will it take to be 75% complete?
  1. 62.1 min
  2. 37.5 min
  3. 31.1 min
  4. 12.9 min
Concept to recallFirst order: k = (2.303/t) log([R]0/[R]). 75% complete = two half-lives.
  1. k = (2.303/10) log(100/80) = 0.2303 × 0.0969 = 0.0223 min−1.
  2. t1/2 = 0.693/0.0223 = 31.1 min.
  3. 75% complete means [R] = [R]0/4, i.e. two half-lives: t = 62.1 min.

Answer: option (1) — 62.1 min

Trap37.5 min treats the reaction as proceeding at a constant rate (zero-order thinking); 31.1 min is only one half-life (50%); 12.9 min puts 75% as the fraction remaining.
Question 13Chemical Kinetics
A zero-order reaction has k = 2.0 × 10−3 mol L−1 s−1. The time taken for the concentration of the reactant to fall from 0.100 M to 0.025 M is
  1. 37.5 s
  2. 25.0 s
  3. 12.5 s
  4. 693 s
Concept to recallZero order: [R] = [R]0 − kt, so t = ([R]0 − [R])/k; t1/2 = [R]0/2k, proportional to [R]0.

  2. Check: the first half-life (0.100 → 0.050) is 25 s, the next (0.050 → 0.025) only 12.5 s.

Answer: option (1) — 37.5 s

Trap25.0 s is only the first half-life; 12.5 s divides the final concentration by k; 693 s applies the first-order formula ln 4/k to a zero-order k.

Question 15Chemical Kinetics
Consider the following statements.
  1. (A) The acid hydrolysis of ethyl acetate in a large excess of water is a pseudo first-order reaction.
  2. (B) A catalyst lowers the activation energy of the forward and backward reactions by the same amount and leaves ΔG of the reaction unchanged.
  3. (C) For a first-order reaction the rate stays the same throughout the reaction.
  4. (D) For the elementary step 2NO + O2 → 2NO2, the order equals the molecularity, 3.
  5. (E) A 10 K rise in temperature roughly doubles the rate mainly because the collision frequency doubles.
Choose the correct answer from the options given below:
  1. (A), (B) and (E) only
  2. (A), (B) and (D) only
  3. (A), (C) and (E) only
  4. (B), (D) and (E) only
Concept to recallFor an elementary step the rate law follows the stoichiometry. Temperature acts through the Boltzmann fraction e−Ea/RT of collisions with E ≥ Ea, not through collision frequency.
  1. (A) true: [H2O] is effectively constant, rate = k′[ester].
  2. (B) true: a catalyst gives a new path, lowers both barriers equally; K and ΔG are unaffected.
  3. (C) false: rate = k[R] falls as [R] falls; only a zero-order rate is constant.

Question 18The d- and f-Block Elements
In aqueous solution Cu+ (3d10) is unstable and disproportionates, 2Cu+(aq) → Cu2+(aq) + Cu(s), even though Cu2+ is 3d9. The reason is that
  1. the second ionisation enthalpy of copper is unusually low
  2. Cu2+ has a completely filled 3d subshell
  3. Cu+ is paramagnetic and therefore highly reactive
  4. the hydration enthalpy of Cu2+ is much more negative than that of Cu+ and more than compensates for the second ionisation enthalpy of copper
Concept to recallThe stability of an ion in water is decided by the whole energy cycle (atomisation, ionisation, hydration), not by its electronic configuration alone. NCERT: the much more negative ΔhydH° of Cu2+(aq) than of Cu+ more than compensates for the second ionisation enthalpy of Cu.
  1. Removing the second electron (3d10 → 3d9) costs a large second ionisation enthalpy.
  2. But the small, doubly charged Cu2+ is hydrated far more strongly than Cu+.
  3. The extra hydration energy outweighs the ionisation cost, so Cu2+(aq) is the stable state and Cu+(aq) disproportionates.

Answer: option (4) — the hydration enthalpy of Cu2+ is much more negative than that of Cu+ and more than compensates for the second ionisation enthalpy of copper

TrapA filled d10 shell (Cu+) is not automatically the stable ion in water; Cu2+ is d9, and Cu+ (d10) is diamagnetic. The second ionisation enthalpy of Cu is high, not low — it is overcome, not avoided.

Question 20The d- and f-Block Elements

  2. Mn3+ → Mn2+ gives the stable half-filled d5, while Cr2+ → Cr3+ gives the stable half-filled t2g3

Question 21Coordination Compounds
[CoF6]3− is paramagnetic while [Co(NH3)6]3+ is diamagnetic. Ignoring pairing energy, their crystal field stabilisation energies are, respectively,
  1. −2.4Δo and −0.4Δo
  2. −1.6Δo and −2.4Δo
  3. −0.4Δo and −2.0Δo
  4. −0.4Δo and −2.4Δo
Concept to recallOctahedral CFSE = (−0.4x + 0.6y)Δo for t2gxegy. Weak field (F−, Δo < P): high spin. Strong field (NH3 with Co3+): low spin.
  1. Co3+ is 3d6.
  2. [CoF6]3−, high spin, t2g4eg2 (4 unpaired): CFSE = −1.6 + 1.2 = −0.4Δo.

  4. N,N-dimethylmethanamine
Concept to recall1° amine → sulphonamide with an acidic N–H, soluble in alkali; 2° → sulphonamide with no N–H, insoluble; 3° → no reaction.

Question 47Biomolecules
When egg albumin is boiled, the protein is denatured. Which statement describes denaturation correctly?
  1. The peptide bonds are hydrolysed and free amino acids are released
  2. Only the primary structure changes; the helix and the folding are unaffected
  3. The protein becomes more active biologically because its helix uncoils
  4. The secondary and tertiary structures are destroyed but the primary structure (amino-acid sequence) stays intact
Concept to recallPrimary structure = sequence (covalent peptide bonds). Secondary (α-helix, β-sheet) and tertiary (folding) are held by H-bonds and other weak forces, which heat or a pH change break.
  1. Heat breaks the H-bonds: the helices uncoil and the globule unfolds; biological activity is lost.
  2. Peptide bonds survive, so the primary structure is unchanged (curdling of milk is another example).

Answer: option (4) — The secondary and tertiary structures are destroyed but the primary structure (amino-acid sequence) stays intact

TrapBreaking peptide bonds is hydrolysis, not denaturation; denaturation always destroys activity.
Question 48Biomolecules
Which of the following is a non-reducing sugar, with its glycosidic linkage correctly described?
  1. Lactose: C1 of β-D-galactose to C4 of β-D-glucose
  2. Sucrose: C1 of α-D-glucose to C4 of β-D-fructose
  3. Sucrose: C1 of α-D-glucose to C2 of β-D-fructose
  4. Maltose: C1 of α-D-glucose to C4 of another α-D-glucose
Concept to recallA disaccharide is reducing if one unit still has a free hemiacetal (or hemiketal) carbon. In sucrose both anomeric carbons (C1 of glucose, C2 of fructose) are used in the link.
  1. Sucrose: C1–C2 link, no free anomeric carbon ⇒ non-reducing.
  2. Maltose and lactose are correctly described but both keep a free C1 on the second glucose ⇒ reducing.
  3. The C1–C4 sucrose option names the wrong carbon of fructose.

Answer: option (3) — Sucrose: C1 of α-D-glucose to C2 of β-D-fructose

TrapThe maltose and lactose links are right; what fails is the “non-reducing” part.
Question 49Biomolecules
Which hormone is correctly described?
  1. Thyroxine — a steroid hormone whose deficiency causes diabetes
  2. Oestradiol — a polypeptide hormone made in the pancreas
  3. Insulin — a polypeptide hormone that lowers blood glucose
  4. Glucagon — a steroid hormone that lowers blood glucose
Concept to recallHormone classes: polypeptides (insulin, glucagon), amino-acid derivatives (thyroxine, adrenaline), steroids (oestradiol, testosterone, cortisol). Insulin lowers and glucagon raises blood glucose.
  1. Insulin is a polypeptide from the pancreas; it lowers blood glucose (its deficiency causes diabetes mellitus).
